Quick answer: this comparison is about two cost structures rather than two places. Century City is mostly sectional title apartments where a levy funds the building and a body corporate decides what gets maintained. Durbanville is mostly freehold houses where every decision and every rand belongs to the owner. One is predictable and constrained, the other is cheaper on average and lumpy in practice.

## What are you actually choosing between?

Two ways of paying for the same thing. Both properties need a roof maintained, an exterior painted and services replaced; the difference is who decides and when the money leaves your account.

| Dimension | Century City apartment | Durbanville house |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maintenance funding | Monthly levy plus a reserve fund | Owner funds it, or does not |
| Who decides | Body corporate trustees | The owner |
| Cost pattern | Predictable, with occasional special levies | Lower on average, lumpy in any year |
| What the buyer inherits | The scheme's past decisions | The seller's past decisions |
| Control | Constrained by conduct rules | Complete |

The pros and cons follow directly. An apartment owner buys predictability and gives up control, which suits someone who does not want to manage a property from a distance. A house owner keeps control and takes on the planning burden, which suits someone who will actually do it. Neither is inherently better and both fail the same way: through deferred maintenance that nobody funded. The [sectional title against freehold guide](/guides/sectional-title-vs-freehold-cape-town/) sets out the legal difference underneath.

## Which one earns more?

Century City, on the ratio, and the reason is unit price rather than rent. Apartments cost less per unit than family houses, and the precinct's office employment supplies tenants year-round without depending on a school calendar.

Durbanville houses let to families choosing a catchment, which produces longer tenancies and lower churn at rents set by what a household can pay rather than by proximity to work. That is a more stable income and a weaker yield, and it comes with a smaller pool: a house vacancy meets fewer qualifying tenants than an apartment vacancy does, so a void runs longer when one opens. Our reading is that Century City suits a landlord optimising for occupancy and Durbanville suits one optimising for quiet, and that the difference in headline yield understates how differently the two behave in a bad year. The [Century City guide](/guides/century-city-property-investment-guide/) and the [Durbanville page](/areas/durbanville-property-investment/) cover each side.

## What does the levy actually buy?

More than most first-time apartment buyers expect, and less than they hope. A levy funds the building: the exterior, the roof, common areas, lifts and services, insurance of the structure, and a reserve for the capital work that arrives on a schedule.

What it does not fund is anything inside the unit, so a buyer still carries their own geyser, fittings and interior. And what it does not guarantee is that the reserve is adequate: a scheme charging a low levy on an ageing building is deferring rather than saving, and the correction arrives as a special levy on whoever owns the unit then. Three documents settle it before an offer, and all three are obtainable: three years of financials showing the reserve balance and its trend, two years of general meeting minutes showing what has been debated, and the current levy schedule. The [levies guide](/guides/sectional-title-levies-cape-town/) covers what a scheme can and cannot recover.

## What does a house owner have to plan for instead?

The same expenditure, without the structure. A Durbanville house has no levy accumulating anything, so the roof, the exterior repaint, the geyser and the driveway are funded by the owner at the moment they fail.

That is genuinely cheaper on average, because nobody is paying a managing agent and no reserve sits idle. It is also the reason freehold buyers most often underestimate their annual cost: a maintenance reserve of even 0.75% of value a year is real money that has to be set aside voluntarily, and most owners do not. Insider tip: ask the seller what has been replaced and in which year, rather than asking about condition. A house with a documented re-roof, a rewire and a recent repaint is a different proposition from one where the answer is vague, and the difference rarely shows in the asking price.

## What do the numbers look like side by side?

A worked comparison at similar capital shows why the two structures diverge over a hold rather than at purchase.

| Line | Century City apartment, R2,600,000 | Durbanville house, R3,900,000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Municipal rates, 2026/27 | about R1,060 a month | about R1,750 a month |
| Levy | R2,200 to R4,000 a month | none |
| Owner-funded maintenance reserve | not required | about 0.75% a year, near R2,440 a month |
| Achievable rent | R16,000 to R19,000 | R21,000 to R25,000 |
| Void when it opens | 3 to 6 weeks | 2 to 3 months |

Both rates figures are worked on the City's current basis at about 0.0064 in the rand, so a R2,400,000 Century City apartment is billed near R11,400 a year and a R4,600,000 Durbanville house near R25,500, each after the same rates-free slice. Because neither suburb's ordinary stock approaches the R8 million valuation at which that slice falls away, the relief applies on both sides and cancels out of the comparison; where it stops cancelling is set out in the [Somerset West and Constantia page](/compare/somerset-west-vs-constantia-investment/). Put those lines back into a yield and the ranking inverts against the price tags. The Century City apartment at R2,400,000 against R16,000 to R19,000 a month models roughly 8.0% to 9.5% gross, compressing to about 4.5% to 5.5% net once the levy, rates, letting commission and a few void weeks come out. The Durbanville house at R4,600,000 against R21,000 to R25,000 models roughly 5.5% to 6.5% gross and about 3.5% to 4.5% net, and its net is the softer of the two mainly because the reserve nobody bills you for is real and the voids run in months rather than weeks. Both figures are modelled from listed prices against observed rents, not audited returns.

The row that decides the comparison is the third: it is optional in practice and not optional in reality, and a Durbanville owner who skips it for 10 years has not saved the money, they have postponed it into one bill. Counted properly, the two structures cost far more alike than the levy line suggests, and the real difference is that one of them enforces the saving.

## Who is the tenant, and how does the year run?

The two suburbs draw different households on different calendars, which matters more to a landlord than the yield gap does.

- **Century City:** professionals working in the precinct's offices and along the northern corridor, on twelve-month leases, re-letting in weeks because the pool renews continuously with employment.
- **Durbanville:** families choosing school catchments, on tenancies that run for years rather than months, re-letting slowly and best marketed before the school year rather than during it.

A landlord who needs the property to let quickly should be in Century City; one who wants to sign a tenant and forget about them should be in Durbanville. The [long-term rental guide](/guides/long-term-rental-cape-town-guide/) covers how lease structure interacts with each pattern.

## Which should you buy?

The decision follows from how much management the owner will actually do, not from which suburb sounds better.

| Priority | Better fit | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Predictable monthly cost | Century City | A levy is a known number |
| Lowest average cost over a decade | Durbanville | No managing agent, no idle reserve |
| Fast re-letting | Century City | Corporate tenant pool renews year-round |
| Long, low-churn tenancies | Durbanville | Families follow a school run |
| Hands-off ownership from abroad | Century City | The scheme maintains the building |
| Full control over the asset | Durbanville | Freehold with no governance above it |

Both sit inside the City of Cape Town, so the rates formula, the valuation roll and the objection calendar are identical on either side of this choice, and the only municipal difference is the valuation each property carries. The [pillar investment guide](/guides/cape-town-property-investment-guide/) covers what a buyer pays to get in.
