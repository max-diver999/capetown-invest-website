Quick answer: off-plan gives you the price today and the building later; resale gives you both today. That is the trade, and everything else follows from it, including the tax. Off-plan from a VAT-registered developer carries no transfer duty because VAT is already inside the price, which on a R3,000,000 purchase is R107,356 you do not pay at registration. What you give up for it is information: the building, the body corporate, the true levy and the finished unit all arrive after the price is agreed.

## What does each price actually include?

The tax difference is structural rather than a concession, and it is worth understanding before treating it as a discount.

A sale by a VAT-registered developer is a VAT supply. Transfer duty does not apply to a transaction that bears VAT, and the VAT is already inside the advertised price, so nothing further is payable at registration on that account. A resale from a private seller is not a VAT supply, so transfer duty applies on the SARS scale, in slices, and is payable in cash before registration.

| Line | Off-plan from a VAT-registered developer | Resale from a private seller |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transfer duty on R3,000,000 | none | R107,356 |
| VAT | already inside the price | not applicable |
| Conveyancing, transfer | often carried by the developer | R38,000 to R45,000 |
| Bond registration if financed | R30,000 to R40,000 | R30,000 to R40,000 |
| When you can let it | on occupation, years away | the month after registration |
| What you can inspect first | plans, a show unit, the developer's record | the actual apartment and the scheme's books |

The duty line is real and it is not a free R107,356. Developers know the saving exists, buyers know it, and it is priced into a market where both sides know it, so a portion of it sits inside the asking price rather than in your pocket. The test is not structural but specific: compare the all-in cost of the actual off-plan unit against the all-in cost of an actual resale unit you would otherwise buy. The [transfer duty guide](/guides/south-africa-transfer-duty-explained/) shows how the scale bites at different price points, and the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-cape-town/) assembles the rest of the entry stack.

## What is the levy going to be, really?

This is the off-plan risk that costs the most and gets discussed the least.

A new scheme's levy is calculated from a budget, and until the first annual general meeting after occupation that budget is effectively the developer's. It is written while units are still being sold, by a party with an interest in the monthly figure looking attractive, using assumptions about insurance, maintenance, management, security and utilities that have never been tested against the actual building.

What follows is predictable. Owners take control at the first meeting, the scheme costs itself honestly, and the levy rises. A buyer who underwrote a yield on the launch figure discovers their net was modelled on a number that was never going to hold.

The defence is simple and rarely applied: underwrite the deal on a levy meaningfully above the launch figure, and ask the developer directly what the budget assumes for insurance, for the managing agent and for the reserve fund contribution. A developer who can answer those three questions precisely is a different proposition from one who cannot. The [levies guide](/guides/sectional-title-levies-cape-town/) sets out what a scheme's levy has to cover once it is being run properly.

## What is the resale hiding?

Its reserve fund, usually, and the answer is in documents rather than in the apartment.

A scheme that has kept levies low by under-funding its reserve looks cheap on the statement and is not. The building still needs its roof, its lifts, its plumbing and its paintwork, and where the reserve does not cover them the money is raised as a special levy from whoever owns the units at that moment. Buy into such a scheme and you have bought a deferred bill along with the apartment.

Three documents settle it before an offer and all three are obtainable: three years of financials showing the reserve balance and the direction it is moving, two years of general meeting minutes, and the maintenance plan read against what has actually been done. A scheme where the minutes record the same repair discussed in three consecutive years is telling you exactly what is coming. The [due diligence guide](/guides/body-corporate-due-diligence-cape-town/) sets out the full document list and how to read each one. Where a new precinct's stock and an established suburb's stock meet the same buyer, the [Century City and Sea Point page](/compare/century-city-vs-sea-point-investment/) works that choice through.

The pros and cons therefore sit on information rather than on price:

- **Off-plan pros:** no transfer duty, a new building with no deferred maintenance, a price agreed before completion, and warranty cover on construction.
- **Off-plan cons:** delivery and developer risk, a launch levy written by the seller, and a price fixed now against a market that does not yet exist.
- **Resale pros:** the building, the scheme's books and the actual unit can all be inspected, and rent starts within weeks of registration.
- **Resale cons:** transfer duty in cash, an existing reserve position you inherit, and a building someone else has been maintaining.

## What does the waiting actually cost?

Less than the usual version of this argument claims, and the corrected version is more useful.

The usual claim is that an off-plan buyer forgoes years of rent. That is only true if their money would otherwise have been fully deployed, and on off-plan it is not: a deposit is commonly around 10% at signing with the balance due on occupation. So the comparison is between two different deployments, not between income and nothing.

| | Resale at R3,000,000 | Off-plan at R3,000,000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash out at the start | about R3,150,000 all-in | about R300,000 deposit |
| Transfer duty | R107,356 | none |
| Conveyancing and Deeds sundries | R39,500 to R48,000 | often carried by the developer |
| Rent in year one | about R237,000 gross, about R180,000 net | none |
| The other R2,700,000 | already in the property | available to earn elsewhere |

Run that honestly and the off-plan buyer is not simply losing two years of income. They are earning nothing on R300,000 while keeping R2,700,000 free, and the resale buyer is earning about R180,000 net on a fully deployed R3,150,000, which is roughly 5.7% on the capital actually committed. Against a prime rate near 10.5%, a bonded resale buyer is paying more for the debt than the asset returns, and an off-plan buyer with cash on deposit is not obviously behind.

So the real cost of off-plan is not foregone rent. It is three specific exposures: the developer may not deliver on time or at all, the levy will be higher than the launch figure, and the price is fixed now against a market that will exist in two or three years. Those are the things to price. A comparison built on rent alone gets the answer for the wrong reason, and a modelled net yield of about 6.0% on City Bowl long-let stock is the number to test each of them against. Both yields here are modelled from listed prices against observed rents rather than audited returns.

## Which one should you buy?

| Situation | Better fit | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income needed soon | Resale | Let from the month after registration |
| Cash constrained at entry | Off-plan | No transfer duty on a VAT-inclusive price |
| Buying from abroad without visiting | Resale | The building and its books can be verified |
| Confident in a specific developer's record | Off-plan | The covenant is the thing you are underwriting |
| Wanting no deferred maintenance | Off-plan | A new building has no reserve deficit to inherit |
| Unwilling to model a levy that will move | Resale | The current levy is a fact, not a projection |
| A long hold either way | Both work | Two years of waiting matters less over fifteen |

The decision comes down to one question asked honestly: can you verify the thing you are buying, or are you underwriting the person selling it? Resale lets you verify. Off-plan asks you to underwrite. Both are legitimate positions, and only one of them is available to a buyer who cannot get the documents.
