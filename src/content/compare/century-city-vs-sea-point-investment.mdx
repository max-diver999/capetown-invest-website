## Century City vs Sea Point: The Core Trade-Off

For a foreign buyer choosing where to put money to work in Cape Town, Century City and Sea Point are not interchangeable. They sit on opposite sides of a single trade-off: location-driven premium income versus modern, managed convenience at a lower entry price. Sea Point is the Atlantic Seaboard income play, built on scarce coastal land, deep tenant demand, and the strongest modeled yields in prime Cape Town. Century City is the master-planned precinct play, built on newer sectional-title stock, professional management, and a more accessible entry point. Get this framing right before comparing listings, because the better choice depends on whether you are buying maximum yield or maximum convenience.

The fault line is income versus ease. Sea Point models roughly 9.7% gross and 7.5% net on a one-bedroom, the top tier of prime Cape Town income, but at a higher price per square metre and with more variable building quality. Century City models around 7.7% gross, slightly lower, but delivers modern buildings, on-site security, and predictable body-corporate management that makes it easier to run as a hands-off buy-to-let. Both nodes share the same powerful tailwind: a Western Cape market up about 179.6% from 2010 to September 2025, the strongest provincial growth in South Africa.

## Yield comparison: Sea Point's income edge

Sea Point models the higher income of the two, roughly 9.7% gross and 7.5% net on a one-bedroom against about 7.7% gross in Century City. The gap is real but it is earned rather than given, because the seaboard number assumes an active owner and a well-run building.

| Yield factor | Sea Point | Century City |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Modeled gross | ~9.7% on a one-bedroom | ~7.7% |
| Modeled net | ~7.5% after costs | Lower, levy-compressed |
| Demand driver | Coastal scarcity, short-let depth | Offices, retail, precinct tenants |
| Levy load | Varies by building age | Higher, funds shared amenities |
| Income character | Premium, location-led | Steady, demand-led |

The headline gap favours Sea Point on raw modeled yield, but the difference is narrower than it looks once you weigh management and risk. Sea Point's 7.5% net reflects premium Atlantic Seaboard demand and the option to run short-stay lets, which lifts gross but adds management intensity. Century City's 7.7% gross comes from a steadier, office-and-retail-anchored tenant base in newer buildings, where lower maintenance risk and professional management offset the slightly lower headline. All figures here are MODELED and directional, not guaranteed; rebuild any model with current rents, levies, and vacancy before you offer. The [Cape Town Rental Yield Guide](/guides/cape-town-rental-yield-guide/) breaks the math down node by node.

Sea Point's 9.7% gross does not arrive as one number, it arrives as a choice between letting strategies. A twelve-month lease to a professional tenant delivers the steadier version of that figure and models near 7.5% net after levies, rates, insurance, and letting commission. Running the same one-bedroom as a short-stay unit lifts peak-season gross well above the long-let line, then hands 20% to 25% of it to an operator and leaves winter weeks unsold. Century City's roughly 7.7% gross works differently: the tenant pool is office and retail staff on annual leases, vacancy tends to be short, and the higher levy that funds precinct security and amenity is the main deduction. The practical read is that Sea Point pays more for active management, while Century City pays a steadier number for very little owner effort.

## Stock and Lifestyle: Coastal Character vs Modern Precinct

The two nodes feel completely different, and that character shapes both tenant demand and management. Sea Point is a dense, walkable coastal suburb on the Atlantic Seaboard, defined by its promenade, sea views, restaurants, and a mix of older blocks, renovated units, and new developments. Its appeal is lifestyle and location: tenants pay a premium to live steps from the sea and the city, which underpins occupancy and supports both long lets and short-stay demand. The trade-off is variable building quality, since stock ranges from dated walk-ups to high-spec new apartments, so the specific building matters enormously.

Century City is the opposite: a purpose-built, master-planned mixed-use precinct combining residential, office, retail, and green space. Its stock is overwhelmingly modern sectional title, with newer buildings, on-site security, managed common areas, and integrated infrastructure. The appeal is convenience, safety, and predictability rather than coastal romance. Tenants are drawn by proximity to offices, the Canal Walk retail centre, and a self-contained, secure environment. For an investor, this translates into lower maintenance surprises and easier management, but the lifestyle premium is functional rather than location-scarce.

| Factor | Sea Point | Century City |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Setting | Coastal Atlantic Seaboard suburb | Master-planned mixed-use precinct |
| Stock | Mixed: older, renovated, new | Modern sectional title |
| Tenant draw | Sea, promenade, walkability | Offices, retail, security |
| Security | Building-dependent | Precinct-wide, on-site |
| Management risk | Higher, varies by building | Lower, professionally managed |

The lifestyle distinction is not cosmetic; it drives the investment case. Sea Point sells scarcity and location, which protects long-term value but demands careful building selection and more active management. Century City sells modern convenience and security, which lowers operational friction and suits hands-off owners, but its growth depends on precinct demand rather than irreplaceable coastal land. Match the stock to your appetite: location-led premium with effort, or modern convenience with ease.

## Tax and foreign-buyer cost: no surcharge either way

Neither node adds a rand to a foreign buyer's cost. South Africa charges no foreign buyer surcharge anywhere, so a non-resident buying in Sea Point or Century City pays the same transfer duty scale as a local. Where the two nodes genuinely differ is price per square metre and the levy structure you inherit afterwards.

Transfer duty is identical in both nodes and it is charged on price, not on nationality. The SARS scale in force since 1 April 2025 exempts the first R1,210,000, then charges 3%, 6%, 8%, 11%, and 13% on the slices above it, with nothing added for a foreign passport. That places South Africa well below the United Kingdom's 2% non-resident stamp duty surcharge and far below Singapore's 60% additional buyer's stamp duty. Because Century City entry prices per square metre sit below Sea Point's, the same budget often lands in a lower duty bracket in the precinct than on the seaboard, which is a real cash difference at transfer. Recurring costs then diverge: Century City levies run higher because they fund shared precinct amenity, while Sea Point levies vary building by building and can carry deferred maintenance a newer scheme has not yet accrued.

| Cost factor | Sea Point | Century City |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foreign buyer surcharge | None | None |
| Transfer duty | Same scale as locals | Same scale as locals |
| Annual wealth tax | None | None |
| Price per square metre | Higher, premium coastal | Lower, modern precinct |
| Levy structure | Varies by building | Higher, amenity-funded |

## Premium Income vs Modern Convenience

The headline gap looks decisive, a modeled 9.7% gross and 7.5% net against roughly 7.7% gross, but the more useful question is how reliably each number is reached in practice.

Sea Point's figure is an average across genuinely variable stock. The strip mixes 1960s and 1970s blocks with recent developments, and the difference between them shows up as levy load, deferred maintenance, and special-levy exposure rather than as anything visible in a listing photograph. A scheme can pass a special levy or a major maintenance decision on a 75% special resolution, and one such vote can absorb a year of net income. Achieving the modeled net in Sea Point means picking the right building, reading the body corporate financials properly, and accepting that the wrong block will underperform the average badly.

Century City's figure comes with tighter dispersion. Newer buildings, consistent management, and predictable levies mean a typical unit lands close to the typical outcome, which is worth something real to an owner who cannot inspect the asset in person or attend a body corporate meeting. The cost of that predictability is the yield gap and exposure to ongoing new supply in the precinct.

Insider tip: in Sea Point, ask for the body corporate's last two audited financials and the minutes of the most recent annual general meeting, not just the current levy figure. Special levies pass on a 75% special resolution, and the warning signs, a thin reserve fund or a deferred roof and lift programme, show up in those minutes long before they show up on a levy invoice. Century City buyers should ask the opposite question: how many units the precinct still has left to deliver.

So the verdict is less about which node wins and more about your capacity to do the work. If you can select buildings carefully and manage actively, Sea Point pays you for it. If you are buying from another continent and want the asset to behave the way the model says, Century City is the more honest fit.

## Closing verification notes

Model both nodes on net yield with the same vacancy and management assumptions, then compare two recent sales in each shortlisted building. A spread above 10% between portal asking prices and deeds-office comps usually means stale listings, not a structural edge for either suburb.
