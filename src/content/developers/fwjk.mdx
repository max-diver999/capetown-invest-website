Quick answer: FWJK develops property and also sells participation in developing it. Its co-development model puts investors into the build at roughly 20% to 25% of project cost, with banks funding the rest, so the return is the developer's margin and the risk is the developer's risk. That is a different product from every other developer on this site.

## What FWJK is

Three businesses in one company, which is the point rather than a footnote.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Core business | Property development |
| Also operates | Architectural studio, quantity surveying practice, estate agency |
| Offices | 5, across Cape Town, Johannesburg and Durban |
| Own projects delivered | More than 41 |
| Professional clients | Growthpoint, Old Mutual, Redefine |

Most developers buy architecture and cost engineering from outside. FWJK employs both, which means design, costing, construction and sale can sit inside one group, with Rolix Estate Agency handling the last step.

That integration cuts both ways for a buyer, and it is worth naming honestly. It compresses the cost of professional services and it removes the independent check that an outside quantity surveyor provides. If the same group prices the build, verifies the cost and sells the result, the buyer's own advisers become the only independent party in the transaction.

Insider tip: the company publishes a staffing breakdown of 14 development managers, 43 architects, 46 cost engineers and 5 project managers against a stated total of 85 professionals. Those figures do not reconcile, which is a small thing, and it is the sort of small thing worth asking about when a developer's numbers are what you are relying on.

## The co-development model, in plain terms

You are not buying a flat. You are buying a slice of a building project.

FWJK's co-development at cost structure lets investors who could not fund a whole development take a proportionate share of one. Each participant commits an amount, remains responsible for their proportion of the project, and receives their share of the gap between actual construction cost and the retail prices the finished units achieve.

The financing is the part that changes the risk profile:

1. **Banks lend up to 80% of total project cost**, on FWJK's account, because they have backed the model since 2008.
2. **Co-developers contribute 20% to 25%** of total cost.
3. **The upside is the developer's margin**, not a property's capital growth.
4. **The downside is the developer's exposure**, including cost overrun, programme delay and slow sales absorption, geared roughly four to one.

FWJK cites bank lending of upwards of R8 billion since 2008 across 38 developments. That is a genuine record and it describes the lenders' view of the company, not a guarantee on the specific scheme you are asked to join.

## Why this belongs in a different mental category

Because the failure modes are not the ones property buyers rehearse.

| | Buying a completed unit | Co-developing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you own | An apartment | A share of a project |
| Main risk | The property market | Cost, programme and absorption |
| Leverage | Your own bond, if any | About 80% of project cost |
| Return comes from | Rent and capital growth | The developer's margin |
| Worst case | The unit is worth less | Your share funds a shortfall |

An investor comfortable with off-plan is not automatically comfortable with this. Off-plan fixes your price and leaves the developer carrying the build; co-development moves that risk onto you in exchange for the margin. The [off-plan guide](/guides/off-plan-property-cape-town-guide/) covers the ordinary route for comparison.

Five questions belong in any conversation about this structure, with any developer. What happens if the build overruns, and who funds it. How are the units priced on sale, and by whom. What are your obligations if the market softens mid-programme. What security do you hold. And what does the exit look like if you need out early.

FWJK's published material also includes a section 12J frequently asked questions set. Incentives of that kind carry sunset provisions, so treat any tax structuring in a developer's own explainer as historical until your own tax adviser confirms the current position.

## The honest summary

Strong record, unusual structure, and a product that needs its own diligence.

The advantages are real: an integrated professional services capability that most developers rent, a lending relationship since 2008 that banks have renewed across 38 projects, more than 41 delivered developments, and a route into development returns that a smaller investor cannot otherwise reach. The disadvantages are equally real: the co-development structure carries construction and absorption risk at roughly 80% leverage, the group's vertical integration removes independent checks a buyer would otherwise get for free, and its own published staffing figures do not add up.

None of that is a recommendation either way. It is the shape of the thing, and anyone considering a co-development stake should bring their own attorney, their own tax adviser and, for a build, their own quantity surveyor.
