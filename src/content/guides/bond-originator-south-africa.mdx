## What a bond originator actually does

You compile one set of documents. The originator submits that package to several banks at the same time, chases each of them, and returns with whatever approvals come back so you can compare them side by side.

That is the whole product. It is not advice about which property to buy and it is not a guarantee of approval. It is a way of running a competitive process instead of a sequential one, which matters because the alternative, applying to banks yourself one after another, leaves you comparing an offer you have today against an offer you might get in a fortnight.

## Who pays, and when

The buyer pays nothing directly. The bank that ultimately grants the bond pays the originator a commission, and it pays only once the bond registers at the Deeds Office. If no bond registers, nobody is paid.

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Who pays the originator | The bank that grants the bond |
| When | On registration, not on application or approval |
| Typical commission | Market practice puts it near 0.5% to 1% of the loan amount |
| What the buyer pays | Nothing directly for the origination service |
| Upfront fees | The National Credit Act prohibits charging an applicant a fee for arranging credit before it is granted |

The practical rule that follows: an originator asking a buyer for money before a bond is granted is doing something the Act does not allow. That is the one red flag worth remembering from this page.

## Why this matters more for a non-resident

For a South African buyer with a salary and a credit record, most banks will look broadly similar. For a buyer living overseas they do not.

A local bank will typically lend a non-resident up to about **50%** of the purchase price, and the remaining 50% has to be introduced from offshore through the banking system. On a R5,000,000 Atlantic Seaboard apartment that is roughly R2,500,000 of local bond against R2,500,000 you bring in. Which banks are comfortable with your income currency, your residence country and your source-of-funds paper is not published anywhere useful, and discovering it by applying one at a time costs weeks you may not have.

| Buyer | Typical local lending ceiling | What the rest must be |
| --- | --- | --- |
| South African resident | Higher, subject to affordability and credit record | Deposit from local funds |
| Non-resident | About 50% of purchase price | Offshore funds introduced through the banking system |

That offshore half is also the part that follows you for years. The record of how the money entered South Africa supports taking proceeds out again when you sell, which is why it is worth building the file properly the first time. The [FICA requirements guide](/guides/fica-requirements-foreign-property-buyers/) covers the verification side of the same paperwork.

## The number your Offer to Purchase has to respect

Approval on a clean, complete file commonly lands in about 3 weeks. Registration after that runs 8 to 12 weeks in a normal transfer, roughly 2 to 3 months, and longer when something goes wrong.

Your [Offer to Purchase](/guides/offer-to-purchase-south-africa/) carries a bond clause with an amount and a deadline, and it is a suspensive condition: if approval does not arrive by that date, the sale lapses. Writing a two-week deadline into an OTP when a non-resident file realistically takes longer is how buyers lose properties on paperwork rather than on price.

Two things to get right in that clause. The amount must reflect what a bank will actually lend you, not what you wish it would, because a condition set above the real ceiling fails. And the deadline must allow for offshore verification, which runs longer than a local application.


| Stage | Typical duration | What it means for your OTP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document pack assembled | Days to weeks for a non-resident | Start before you make an offer, not after |
| Bond approval on a clean file | About 3 weeks | A 2 week bond deadline is not survivable |
| Registration after approval | 8 to 12 weeks, roughly 2 to 3 months | Sets your realistic occupation date |
| When a file goes wrong | Past 4 months | Municipal arrears, a lost title deed, a missing bond cancellation notice |

Two more figures shape what you can promise in the contract. Non-resident lending sits near **50%** of price, so the deposit you must show is the other **50%**, introduced from offshore. And on the tax side the price line triggers either transfer duty above the zero-rated threshold or **15%** VAT on a new build, never both, which changes the cash you need on the same day the bond funds.

## What slows a non-resident file

| Cause | What to do about it |
| --- | --- |
| Source-of-funds evidence missing or thin | Assemble it before you apply, not after the bank asks |
| Income in a currency the bank is uncomfortable with | Ask the originator which lenders accept it before submitting |
| Proof of address from another jurisdiction | Expect certified copies and allow time |
| Piecemeal applications made directly | Multiple separate credit enquiries without a clean comparison |

## Advantages and disadvantages of using one

Neither route is automatically right. The comparison is short enough to make honestly.

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bond originator | One application reaches several banks; no direct cost to you; surfaces lenders you have no relationship with; useful when non-resident policy is opaque | You do not control which banks are approached; the originator earns only on a registered bond, so its interest is in a deal closing; service quality varies |
| Applying to your own bank | An existing private banking relationship can price sharper; you keep the relationship in one place; direct contact with a decision maker | One offer with nothing to compare it against; no help if that bank declines a non-resident profile |
| Applying to several banks yourself | Full control | Repeated credit enquiries; offers that expire at different times; weeks lost running them sequentially |

For a buyer in South Africa with a strong existing bank relationship, going direct is defensible. For a buyer offshore, where the practical question is which lender will look at your profile at all, the originator route usually answers that faster and at no cost.

## Originator or your own bank

Both routes are legitimate and they are not exclusive.

An existing private banking relationship sometimes prices better than a general application, because the bank already holds your assets and knows your profile. An originator submission covers banks you have no relationship with at all. Running both in parallel and taking the better outcome is a reasonable strategy, and costs you nothing beyond the paperwork.

What to avoid is applying separately to several banks yourself over several weeks. That generates repeated credit enquiries, gives you offers that expire at different times, and leaves you without the side-by-side comparison that made the exercise worth doing.
