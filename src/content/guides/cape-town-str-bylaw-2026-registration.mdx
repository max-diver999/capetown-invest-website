## What does Cape Town's draft short-term letting by-law require?

The draft Short-Term Letting By-law requires registration of every property offered for short-term letting on any booking platform, a City registration number on each listing, and availability data shared with the City. Published in August 2026, it reaches roughly 17,889 active listings, and foreign buyers hold a meaningful share of the stock earning R1,600 to R2,900 a night.

Three mechanisms do the work:

- **Registration.** Each short-let property registers with the City and receives a number that must appear on every advert and platform listing.
- **Data sharing.** Platforms, owners and managing operators must hand the City listing availability data, giving the Revenue Department a matching tool it has never had.
- **Rates alignment.** Properties running commercially move onto commercial rates, closing the gap the City says the existing Rates Policy already intended.

The stated purpose is compliance with the Rates Policy rather than a cap on tourism. Cape Town remains the country's largest short-let market, and the City has said repeatedly that it wants the sector formalised, not shut.

Registration regimes of this kind change who profits, and Cape Town Invest expects the effect to arrive through the rates bill rather than the booking calendar. A dedicated short-let flat billed as residential today enjoys the R620,000 rates-free portion and a residential rate-in-rand of roughly 0.0064; reclassified as commercial, it loses the exemption and pays a materially higher rate per rand of value. On a R3,000,000 apartment that swing can exceed R15,000 a year before a single guest checks in, which is the difference between a modelled 5% net yield and one closer to 4.5%. Demand is not the issue: median occupancy sat near 71% in early 2026. Cost discipline is, and buyers who underwrite the commercial figure now will not be surprised by the reclassification letter later.

## Who has to register, and what counts as commercial letting?

Registration under the draft applies to every property listed on a booking platform, from a full-time Airbnb apartment in Sea Point to a spare room, and foreign buyers register on the same terms as residents. The commercial rates consequence is narrower: it targets properties offering room-nights for short-term letting at least 50% of the time.

| Situation | Must register | Commercial rates exposure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dedicated Airbnb flat, listed year-round | Yes | High: over the 50% availability line |
| Primary home let during owner's travel | Yes | Low: under 50% of room-nights |
| Spare room let most weekends | Yes | Low to moderate: depends on availability share |
| Guesthouse with staff and signage | Yes, plus land-use consent | Already commercial in most cases |

Our analysis of the draft's structure suggests the 50% test is the line every investor should price. A buyer planning 11 months of continuous listing sits squarely in the commercial category; a semigration family letting their home for 8 weeks a year does not. The draft asks about availability offered, not occupancy achieved, so a poorly performing full-time listing still counts as commercial.

## How does the 50% room-night rule change your rates bill?

Commercial rating costs real money because Cape Town bills commercial property at a higher rate-in-rand and without the residential rates-free portion. From 1 July 2026 the budget exempts the first R620,000 on homes up to R8 million, a line foreign buyers of dedicated short-lets stand to lose entirely under the draft.

A worked example shows the stakes. Take a R3,000,000 Sea Point one-bedroom run as a dedicated short-let:

- **Residential rating:** rates are charged on R2,380,000 after the R620,000 exemption, at the residential rate-in-rand of roughly 0.0064, near R15,200 a year.
- **Commercial rating:** the exemption falls away and the commercial rate-in-rand runs at a multiple of residential, so the annual rates line can more than double.
- **Levy interaction:** the body corporate levy does not change, but a scheme's conduct rules may, which is a separate risk covered in our [body corporate Airbnb rules guide](/guides/body-corporate-airbnb-ban-rules/).

Model the commercial figure into net yield before you buy. The [Cape Town rates and taxes guide](/guides/cape-town-rates-taxes-property/) walks through the full 2026/27 rates mechanics, and the by-law would simply push dedicated short-lets into the more expensive column of a system that already exists.

## What are the penalties for ignoring the by-law?

Contravention of the draft by-law carries a fine or imprisonment of up to 6 months, and the enforcement mechanism is built into the data-sharing duty rather than left to inspectors. Platforms, owners and operators must all supply availability data, which means the City can match every registered and unregistered listing against its own rates records at desk level.

Enforcement in practice is likely to follow the revenue path:

1. Listings scraped or supplied by platforms are matched to property addresses.
2. Properties over the 50% availability line billed as residential get reclassified to commercial rates.
3. Backdated rates adjustments and penalties land on the owner's municipal account.
4. Persistent non-registration escalates to prosecution under the by-law's penalty clause.

Data sharing converts enforcement from a door-knocking exercise into a database query, and that is what makes this draft different from earlier attempts to police short-letting. Platforms must report availability, owners must register, and the City already knows the rates category of all 970,000 properties on its valuation roll. Matching the three lists exposes every dedicated short-let billed as residential, and the draft's penalty clause, a fine or up to 6 months imprisonment, is the backstop rather than the main event. The realistic financial exposure is a backdated reclassification: commercial rates applied from the date the City can show commercial use began, plus interest on the shortfall. An owner who listed year-round through 2025 and 2026 could face an adjustment covering 24 months, and municipal debt attaches to the property account, surfacing in the rates clearance certificate at the next sale.

Insider tip: the municipal account follows the property, not the host. A buyer who takes transfer of a flat with an unresolved reclassification inherits the dispute, so ask for the seller's rates account history and any correspondence about letting categorisation during due diligence, the same way you would check for an undisclosed special levy.

## How does the by-law interact with zoning and body corporate rules?

Registration becomes the fifth layer of short-let compliance for foreign buyers, not a replacement for the existing four. Zoning still limits transient letting to 30 consecutive days per guest, a body corporate can still restrict short stays by 75% special resolution, and a multi-room guesthouse still requires land-use consent.

The full stack after adoption would read:

| Layer | Rule that binds you | Where it can kill the model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zoning | Transient stays up to 30 days; guesthouses need consent | Multi-room operations without land-use approval |
| By-law registration | City number on every listing, data shared | Unregistered listings face fines and reclassification |
| Body corporate | Conduct rules can restrict short stays by 75% special resolution | Scheme bans nightly letting after you buy |
| Insurance | Standard household cover excludes paying guests | Uninsured guest damage or liability claim |
| SARS | Rental income taxable, resident or not | Undeclared income surfacing through data sharing |

Worked example of the interaction: a foreign owner registers a De Waterkant loft under the by-law, but the scheme then passes a conduct rule restricting stays under 30 days. Registration does not override the scheme. The [short-term rental rules guide](/guides/short-term-rental-rules-cape-town/) covers the four existing layers in detail, and the by-law changes none of them.

## What is the timeline from draft to enforcement?

Public participation on the draft closes on 5 October 2026, which leaves about 6 weeks to shape the final text. The City then considers submissions, amends the draft where it accepts them, and takes the by-law to Council for adoption before gazetting a commencement date, realistically during 2027.

Cape Town Invest reads the realistic sequence as:

- **August to 5 October 2026:** comment window open; industry bodies and hosts lobby on the 50% test and data-sharing scope.
- **Late 2026:** Council adoption of a final text, possibly softened on data-sharing mechanics.
- **During 2027:** commencement notice, registration system opens, first compliance sweep against platform data.

Buyers negotiating a short-let purchase in this window hold a genuine information edge: the seller's yield story is priced on the old rules, while the cost base is about to change. Underwrite on commercial rates and treat any residential-rated projection for a dedicated short-let as a claim to verify, not a fact.

## What should owners and buyers do before the comment window closes?

Preparation beats waiting, because every step below is worth doing even if the final by-law softens. Owners of dedicated short-lets carry the most exposure and have the most to gain from commenting; the submission process is open to anyone until 5 October 2026 through the City's public participation channels.

The pre-adoption checklist Cape Town Invest recommends:

- Submit a comment if the 50% availability test or data-sharing scope affects your model.
- Pull your municipal account and confirm which rates category the property currently carries.
- Recalculate net yield with commercial rates for any property offered over 50% of room-nights.
- Read your scheme's registered conduct rules and the last 2 AGM minutes for pending short-let motions.
- File your platform statements against SARS declarations, since data sharing makes mismatches visible.

Buyers mid-purchase should add one suspensive step: make the offer conditional on reviewing the rates account and the scheme's conduct rules, which costs nothing and prices the by-law risk before transfer rather than after.

## Which buyer profile should care most about this by-law?

Dedicated short-let investors carry the full weight of the change, and our analysis says the by-law barely touches everyone else. Short-let economics remain strong, with median occupancy near 71% and average daily rates from R1,600 to R2,900 across the Atlantic Seaboard and City Bowl belt, so the question is cost, not demand.

Detailed short-let economics sit in the [Airbnb investment guide](/guides/airbnb-investment-cape-town-guide/), and the highest-exposure buildings cluster along the [Sea Point strip](/areas/sea-point-property-investment/), where nightly letting is densest.

Three profiles, three answers:

1. **Full-time Airbnb investor:** model commercial rates and registration compliance now; the [suburb-by-suburb Airbnb yield guide](/guides/airbnb-yields-by-suburb-cape-town/) shows where the numbers still clear after the extra cost.
2. **Hybrid owner** using the property personally and letting the rest: the under-50% carve-out is designed for you, but keep evidence of your own occupancy.
3. **Long-let landlord:** no registration category applies to leases of a month or longer, and tenant demand may firm as marginal short-lets convert to long-let stock.

Commenting before 5 October 2026 matters even if you are not yet letting. Owners who register objections on data-sharing scope and the 50% test shape the final enforcement cost base. Buyers should read submitted industry comments to gauge how harsh commercial reclassification will be in 2027.

Platforms will eventually display registration numbers, but scheme conduct rules will still decide whether you may list at all. Underwrite short-let stock on the strictest layer first, then add City registration cost, then model revenue. That order prevents buying a property that is legal with the City but forbidden by the body corporate.

Hybrid owners letting fewer than 50% of room-nights should keep a simple log: dates you slept in the unit, utility consumption pattern, and platform calendar exports. The City's carve-out is availability-based, not intent-based. A Sea Point owner who blocks six weeks for personal use and lists 20 weeks can retain residential rates; a dedicated operator on 45 weeks faces commercial rating even before counting revenue.
