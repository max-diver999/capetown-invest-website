## How do returning expats fund a Cape Town property purchase?

Three common funding buckets: offshore savings earned after leaving usually flow through an authorised dealer with non-resident endorsement if you remain non-tax-resident; sale of foreign property follows the same dealer channel with FICA source-of-funds checks taking 14 to 28 business days for amounts above R5 million; retirement and emigrant benefits may require SARB-approved paths if you formally emigrated under old rules before tax emigration replaced the process. Historical formal emigration closed as a SARB mechanism, but legacy blocked accounts persist for many expats who left in the 2000s or early 2010s. ## When does buying Cape Town property make you a South African tax resident?

Buying a Cape Town property does not automatically make you tax resident, but living here for more than 91 days in the current tax year and more than 915 days over five years usually does under the physical presence test. The ordinarily resident test also counts: if South Africa remains your permanent home base even while you work abroad temporarily, SARS may treat you as resident regardless of day count. Relocating to Cape Town with a local employment contract almost always triggers tax residency, while buying a flat you visit for four weeks per year typically keeps you non-resident. If you let the unit while abroad, SA rental tax may apply even before relocation through withholding rules for non-resident landlords, so declare through a tax practitioner before you sign the first lease.

Buying does not make you tax resident. Living here usually does.

## Can returning expats get a South African home loan from abroad?

Yes, but the terms turn on which side of the resident line your bank places you. A South African citizen with no local income and no SA employment is assessed under non-resident rules, which caps the loan near 50% of value and leaves the balance to come from offshore. The same person holding three months of South African payslips is usually assessed as a local applicant, and returning citizens at that point often reach 70% to 80% loan-to-value.

That gap is the largest single variable in a return budget, so sequencing matters more than rate shopping. Some banks apply the non-resident cap until payslips exist, which pushes a bonded purchase to after your start date rather than before it. Others will consider an employment contract with a start date a few months out. Ask the specific question before you sign, because a suspensive bond clause written assuming 80% and granted at 50% leaves you finding the difference inside the conditional period.

Credit history is the other lever. Dormant South African accounts and closed facilities slow approval, and a buyer who shut everything on leaving can wait 45 to 60 days for a new facility. Refresh your local credit report around six months before you intend to apply.

## What transfer duty and costs do returning expats pay on Cape Town resale?

Exactly what any other buyer pays. A South African passport earns no discount and carries no penalty. Resale property runs on the same SARS sliding scale, with nothing due below R1,210,000 and rates stepping from 3% up to 13% on the slice above R13,310,000. New stock bought directly from a VAT-registered developer carries 15% VAT in the price instead of duty, which is why a new-build price list and a resale asking price are not directly comparable.

The figures move quickly with price. Duty on a R4.1m Durbanville resale adds roughly R280,000. On a R7.8m Constantia townhouse it adds roughly R520,000. Conveyancing, bond registration where you finance, and Deeds Office charges sit on top of that, and the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-cape-town/) sets out each line.

The cost returning buyers most often underestimate is the carry rather than the duty. Levies on the sectional title stock expats favour averaged R3,400 a month on a R2.8m Atlantic Seaboard one-bed sample, with rates running alongside. Buy before you sell abroad and that carry stacks on top of rent you are still paying: R28,000 a month in rental plus R18,000 on a vacant owned flat passes R500,000 over twelve months.

## What are the pros, cons, and typical return scenarios for expat buyers?

Returning on an SA passport removes frictions that pure foreign buyers face, and adds two of its own. The upside is bond access: local payslips typically lift lending to 70% to 80% of value against the roughly 50% cap applied to non-residents. The downside is exchange control, which gets harder rather than easier if you formally emigrated.

Pros:

- FICA usually clears faster on an SA ID than on a foreign passport and address pack.
- Bond access improves sharply once local payslips exist, at 70% to 80% loan-to-value against the roughly 50% non-resident cap.
- You already understand sectional title, levies, and how the local market describes itself.
- Hard-currency savings earned abroad stretch further against a rand purchase price.

Cons:

- Exchange control gets more complicated, not less, if you formally emigrated under the old SARB process and still hold legacy blocked assets.
- Tax residency can reattach faster than expected through the presence test, at more than 91 days in the current year and 915 days across five.
- Nostalgia pricing is real. A first-weekend-back purchase in a childhood suburb rarely survives comparison with a similar unit priced 10% lower.

The mandates cluster into three shapes. A London seller converting a UK house sale, budgeting 14 to 28 business days of source-of-funds review on amounts above R5 million. A Gauteng semigrator trading a Pretoria house for larger Durbanville stock and keeping a cash buffer. An empty-nester buying near healthcare with no school catchment constraint, and therefore no timing pressure at all.
