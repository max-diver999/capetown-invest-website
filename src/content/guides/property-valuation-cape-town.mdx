## Which number are you actually asking for?

Three, and they answer different questions. An owner asking what the property is worth usually wants the third and is quoted the first, which is where most of the confusion starts.

| Number | Who produces it | What it is for | What it costs you if wrong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Municipal valuation | City of Cape Town, mass valuation | Calculating rates | A monthly overpayment until corrected |
| Bank valuation | The lender's valuer | Securing a bond | A smaller loan than expected |
| Market value | A marketed sale | Selling or buying | Time on market, or money left behind |

The municipal figure is the one with an ongoing cost attached, because rates are billed against it every month whether or not you agree with it. The bank figure only matters when you borrow. Market value only becomes real when you sell.

## Why does the municipal valuation differ from market value?

Because it is a mass valuation. The City values hundreds of thousands of properties as at a single date using models and available sales data, not by sending a valuer into each house. That method is the only practical way to rate a metro, and it necessarily misses things that are specific to one property.

Three situations produce the widest gaps. Recently transacted stock, where the roll reflects a valuation date that predates the sale. Properties with a defect or a constraint the model cannot see, such as damp, a servitude, or difficult access. And properties whose recorded extent, use or improvements do not match reality, which is a factual error rather than a judgement.

Insider tip: check what the roll says about your property, not just the value. The recorded erf extent, the use category and the list of improvements are all on the record, and an error in any of them is the strongest possible basis for an objection because it is a fact rather than an opinion.

## How do you object, and what works?

During the objection period the City opens when a general valuation roll is published, and through the query and appeal routes outside it. What decides an objection is evidence, and not all evidence is equal.

| What you submit | How strong it is |
| --- | --- |
| A factual error in extent, use or improvements | Strongest, it is a correction rather than an argument |
| A defect with a professional report and a cost | Strong, the model could not have seen it |
| Comparable sales in the same street and period | Strong, if genuinely comparable |
| An agent's opinion of value | Weak on its own |
| The price you paid | Useful, but only in context of the valuation date |

The [municipal valuation objection guide](/guides/cape-town-municipal-valuation-objection-gv/) sets out the process and the timing in detail. The point worth carrying here is that the objection is about the valuation date rather than about today, so evidence has to speak to what the property was worth then.

The arithmetic is worth doing before you decide whether to object. Rates are the valuation less the R620,000 relief, multiplied by roughly 0.64 cents in the rand. A property over-valued by R500,000 therefore overpays about R3,200 a year, or R267 a month, every month until the roll is corrected. Over a five-year general valuation cycle that is around R16,000 on a single error.

There is a second reason to care about the roll beyond the monthly bill. The City's residential rates relief of R620,000 is a deduction from the valuation, and it falls away entirely above R8 million, so a property valued just over that line pays materially more than one valued just under it. On stock sitting near the threshold, the roll figure is worth reading carefully rather than accepting. The [rates and taxes guide](/guides/cape-town-rates-taxes-property/) works through what the step costs.

## What does a wrong valuation actually cost?

Enough to be worth an afternoon. Rates are the valuation less the R620,000 relief, multiplied by roughly 0.64 cents in the rand, so every R100,000 of over-valuation costs about R640 a year, or R53 a month, for as long as the roll stands.

| Valuation on the roll | Rateable after R620,000 relief | Rates a year | Rates a month |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R2,000,000 | R1,380,000 | R8,832 | R736 |
| R3,000,000 | R2,380,000 | R15,232 | R1,269 |
| R5,000,000 | R4,380,000 | R28,032 | R2,336 |
| R8,000,000 | R7,380,000 | R47,232 | R3,936 |
| R8,100,000 | R8,100,000, no relief | R51,840 | R4,320 |
| R10,000,000 | R10,000,000, no relief | R64,000 | R5,333 |

Two more numbers frame that table. The City runs a general valuation on a multi-year cycle, so a roll figure you accept in 2026 can still be billing you in 2029, which turns a small monthly error into a four-figure one. And the residential tariff of 0.64 cents in the rand is 0.64% of rateable value a year, which is the cleanest way to sanity-check any rates figure you are quoted.

Look at rows four and five. R100,000 of extra valuation across the R8 million line adds about R4,600 a year rather than the R640 the tariff alone would suggest, because the R620,000 relief disappears entirely rather than tapering. On stock valued between roughly R7.8 million and R8.2 million, the roll figure is worth more scrutiny than anywhere else in the metro.

## Why is the bank's number lower?

Because a lender is not valuing your home, it is valuing its own security. The question a bank valuer answers is what the property would realise if the bank had to sell it to recover the debt, which deliberately excludes the buyer who pays a premium because the light is right.

That produces a systematic gap rather than an error, and it is the reason a bond approval can come in below what an agent said the house was worth. For a buyer the practical consequence is the deposit: a bank lending a percentage of its own valuation rather than of the purchase price leaves the difference for the buyer to fund in cash. Non-residents feel this twice over, because lending to them is typically capped near 50% loan-to-value before the valuation question even arises. On a R4 million purchase that is R2 million of cash before costs, against a local buyer who might put down 10%. The [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-cape-town/) sets out what the banks actually ask for.

## What actually moves value in Cape Town?

Land and position first, then condition, then everything owners tend to overestimate. In freehold stock the erf and where it sits do most of the work: Constantia is priced on land rather than on the house standing on it, which is why its modelled yields sit under 4%. In sectional title the floor, the aspect, the parking and the view do more than the finishes.

Rand per square metre is the comparison that travels. The metro runs from roughly R30,000 per m² in the Northern Suburbs to R55,000 per m² at the Atlantic Seaboard's entry and past R180,000 per m² in Clifton, a spread of about 6 times across 40 minutes of driving. A 120 m² apartment at R55,000 per m² is a R6.6 million property; the same 120 m² in Durbanville is nearer R3.6 million. Comparing two properties on total price without dividing by area is the most common valuation error an owner makes.

Three things move value less than owners expect. High-specification renovation in a modest street rarely recovers its cost, because the street sets a ceiling. Cosmetic upgrades fade within a few years and are re-done by the next owner anyway. And additions that were built without approved plans reduce value rather than adding it, because an unapproved structure becomes the buyer's liability and shows up in due diligence.

The things that reliably protect value are unglamorous: a sound roof, dry walls, compliant electrical and plumbing work, and a scheme with a funded reserve if the property is sectional title. Those are also, not coincidentally, what the compliance certificates test at transfer, which the [certificates guide](/guides/cost-of-selling-compliance-certificates/) covers.

## Where do you get real comparable data?

From recorded sales rather than from asking prices, which is the distinction that makes most online estimates unreliable. An asking price is an opinion; a recorded transfer is a fact.

Two sources do the work. Deeds Office records show what actually transferred and at what price, which is the underlying truth. Aim for sales within 6 months and within the same street or block, because a comparable from 18 months ago in a different pocket is a different market. Commercial data providers aggregate those records into suburb-level trends, which is what our [market data guide](/guides/cape-town-property-market-data-lightstone/) draws on. For the price bands by suburb across the metro, the [suburb price table](/guides/cape-town-property-prices-by-suburb-2026/) sets out rand per square metre and entry tickets, and the [suburb map](/guides/cape-town-suburb-map/) places each suburb in its corridor.

Insider tip: when you compare comparables, match the erf size and the floor area before you match the suburb. Two Rondebosch houses at the same price can sit on erven that differ by half, and the one on the larger erf is the better asset even if the house is worse, because land is the part that does not depreciate.
