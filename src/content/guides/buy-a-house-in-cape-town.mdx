## What actually happens, in order?

Eight steps, and only the first two are negotiable. Everything after the seller signs runs on a process controlled by the conveyancer, the bank and the Deeds Office rather than by either party.

| Step | Who drives it | Typical time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer to purchase signed by both parties | Buyer and seller | The contract exists from here |
| Suspensive conditions met, usually bond approval | Buyer and bank | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Conveyancer instructed, FICA on both parties | Transferring attorney | Runs in parallel |
| Rates clearance certificate applied for | Attorney and the City | 2 to 6 weeks, a common delay |
| Compliance certificates obtained | Seller | Before lodgement |
| Transfer duty or VAT paid to SARS | Buyer, through the attorney | Before lodgement |
| Documents lodged at the Deeds Office | Attorney | 1 to 3 weeks in the office |
| Registration, keys, occupation | Deeds Office | Transfer complete |

The 8 to 12 weeks is measured from the end of the second row, not from the first. A buyer who signs in March and waits four weeks for bond approval is registering in July, and the most common cause of slipping past 12 weeks is not the Deeds Office but the rates clearance figure. The [transfer timeline guide](/guides/property-transfer-timeline-delays/) covers what stalls and how long each stall usually runs.

## Why is the offer to purchase the whole negotiation?

Because South African law requires a sale of land to be in writing and signed by both parties, and once that happens there is a binding contract. There is no general cooling-off period on most purchases, so a buyer who signs and then discovers a problem is relying on whatever conditions they wrote in beforehand.

Four conditions belong in almost every offer, and each one is a right you lose by omission:

1. **Bond approval**, with an amount and a date, so the sale falls away if finance does not arrive rather than leaving you in breach.
2. **A satisfactory inspection**, defined by outcome rather than by opinion, so the clause is enforceable.
3. **Sight of the body corporate financials and minutes** on sectional title, with a period to withdraw if they disclose a special levy.
4. **A long-stop date**, after which either party can walk if transfer has not happened.

Insider tip: put the occupation date and occupational rent in the offer explicitly. If transfer runs late and the buyer has already moved in, or the seller stays on after registration, the amount payable is whatever the contract says, and a blank there becomes an argument at exactly the point when neither party wants one.

## What does it cost above the purchase price?

Transfer duty, conveyancing, Deeds Office fees, and bond costs if you are financing. Transfer duty is nil below R1,210,000 and then rises through bands, starting at 3% on the portion from R1,210,001 to R1,663,800 and 6% on the portion to R2,329,300, reaching 13% on everything above R13,310,000.

The rule buyers most often get wrong is that VAT and transfer duty are alternatives, never both. A new build bought directly from a VAT-registered developer carries 15% VAT, normally inside the quoted price, and no transfer duty at all. A resale from an ordinary seller carries transfer duty and no VAT. Confirm which one applies in writing before you sign, because a price quoted excluding VAT raises the real cost by a sixth.

The [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-cape-town/) sets out the full stack with worked examples, and the [transfer duty guide](/guides/south-africa-transfer-duty-explained/) covers the bands. Get an actual quote from the transferring attorney rather than relying on a generic calculator, because the conveyancing fee scales with price and the bond registration cost is a separate instruction to a separate attorney.

## Which certificates does the seller owe?

An electrical compliance certificate is required on transfer nationally, and Cape Town adds a plumbing certificate by municipal by-law. A beetle certificate is customary in the Western Cape and contracted for rather than legislated, and gas and electric fence certificates apply where those installations exist.

| Certificate | Required by | Usual payer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrical compliance | National regulation, on transfer | Seller, by convention |
| Plumbing | Cape Town municipal by-law | Seller, by convention |
| Beetle | Western Cape custom, contract-driven | Seller, if the contract says so |
| Gas | Where a gas installation exists | Seller, by convention |
| Electric fence | Where an electric fence exists | Seller, by convention |

Convention is not law, and the contract decides. Read the clause rather than assuming, because a seller who has struck the beetle certificate out of the standard wording has told you something about the property as well as about the negotiation. The [compliance certificates guide](/guides/cost-of-selling-compliance-certificates/) sets out what each inspection actually covers and what a failed certificate costs to remedy.

## What changes if you are buying from abroad?

The law does not change; the logistics do. A foreign national buys, registers and owns on the same basis as a South African, with no surcharge, no minimum spend and no visa requirement.

Three practical differences shape the timeline. Financing is capped for most non-residents near 50% loan-to-value, so the cash requirement is roughly double a local buyer's on the same house. FICA verification takes longer with foreign documents, and starting it early rather than at the offer stage removes a common delay. And purchase funds should be introduced through an authorised dealer bank with the title deed endorsed non-resident, which costs nothing at the time and protects the clean repatriation of the proceeds when you sell.

A buyer who cannot attend can complete the whole transaction on a properly drawn power of attorney, which the [remote purchase guide](/guides/buy-cape-town-property-remotely/) and the [power of attorney guide](/guides/power-of-attorney-property-south-africa/) cover between them. The [foreign buyer guide](/guides/buy-cape-town-property-foreigner/) is the starting point for the wider position.

That is a different question from how to buy, and it is answered by corridor rather than by suburb. The metro divides into nine, and the entry price runs from about R30,000 per square metre in the Northern Suburbs to past R180,000 in Clifton.
