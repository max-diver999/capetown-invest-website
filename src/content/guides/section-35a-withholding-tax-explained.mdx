## What is section 35A and when does it apply?

Section 35A of the Income Tax Act is a collection mechanism: it requires the buyer, through the conveyancer, to withhold a slice of the purchase price whenever the seller is a non-resident and the price exceeds R2 million. The withheld amount reaches SARS within 21 days of registration and stands as a credit against the seller's capital gains tax.

The test has exactly two legs, and both must be present:

- **Non-resident seller:** tax residency decides, not citizenship or passport; an emigrated South African is caught, a foreign national who became SA tax resident is not.
- **Price above R2 million:** the threshold reads the full price, so R2,000,000 even escapes and R2,050,000 is withheld on in full.
- **Any property type:** houses, sectional title units, vacant land and shares deriving value from SA property all fall inside the net.

Foreign buyers meet the section from the other side too: when they eventually sell, the same machinery applies to them, which is why Cape Town Invest models the exit withholding into every purchase underwrite from day one. Where it sits among the certificates, commission and repatriation steps is mapped in the [full non-resident selling guide](/guides/selling-property-south-africa-non-resident/).

## How much does section 35A take from your sale?

The withholding rate follows the seller's legal form: 7.5% of the full price for an individual, 10% for a company and 15% for a trust, with no deduction for bonds, costs or the original purchase price. Sized on the price rather than the gain, it routinely overshoots the real tax.

| Sale price | Individual 7.5% | Company 10% | Trust 15% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R2,500,000 | R187,500 | R250,000 | R375,000 |
| R4,500,000 | R337,500 | R450,000 | R675,000 |
| R8,000,000 | R600,000 | R800,000 | R1,200,000 |
| R15,000,000 | R1,125,000 | R1,500,000 | R2,250,000 |

Three things the standard withholding ignores entirely:

- **Your bond:** the bank is settled from the remaining proceeds, not before the withholding is taken.
- **Your base cost:** what you paid and improved counts only at assessment, or through a directive.
- **Your actual gain:** a sale at a loss still suffers the full withholding without an NR03.

The cash-flow shock lands hardest where a bond consumed most of the price. A worked example: an individual sells at R4.5 million with a R3.2 million bond to settle, agent commission of R311,000 including VAT and a real capital gain of R400,000 after base costs. The true CGT at 40% inclusion and a 45% marginal rate is roughly R64,800, yet the standard withholding takes R337,500 at 7.5% of the full price, more than 5 times the actual liability, and the seller banks R650,000 less at registration than the deal economics suggest. After settling the bond and commission, barely R650,000 of a R4.5 million sale reaches the seller's account on day one. Every rand of the excess does come back after assessment, but months later and interest-free, which is precisely the gap the NR03 directive exists to close before transfer.

## How does the NR03 directive reduce the withholding?

A tax directive is SARS's pre-approval of a lower withholding, applied for on form NR03 before registration with a computation of the actual expected gain. Where the calculation shows tax below the standard 7.5% to 15% take, SARS instructs the conveyancer to withhold the lower figure, or nothing on a genuine loss.

The directive process in practice:

1. **Prepare the computation:** purchase price, capitalised improvements with invoices, selling costs and the resulting gain.
2. **Lodge NR03 with SARS:** through the conveyancer or a tax practitioner, ideally the week the sale agreement is signed.
3. **SARS responds:** with a directive stating the amount to withhold, which the conveyancer follows at registration.
4. **Registration proceeds:** the reduced amount goes to SARS within 21 days; the balance of the price reaches you immediately.

Timing is the whole game: our analysis of seller files puts a well-prepared directive at 2 to 6 weeks for a response, comfortably inside an 8 to 12 week transfer, while a late application forces a choice between delaying registration and accepting the full withholding. Sellers at a loss, sellers with small gains and trusts facing the 15% rate gain the most, and the [capital gains guide](/guides/south-africa-capital-gains-tax-property/) shows the base-cost records that make the computation stick.

## Who carries the liability when section 35A is missed?

Liability under section 35A lands on the buyer first: a purchaser who fails to withhold the 7.5% to 15% from a non-resident seller above R2 million becomes personally liable for the amount, which is why no professionally run transfer skips the residency question. Foreign buyers meet the same exposure from the purchase side of their own deals.

The statutory duties run in a chain, and the numbers make it self-enforcing. Estate agents and conveyancers who are entitled to remuneration from the transaction and who know, or reasonably should know, that the seller is a non-resident must notify the buyer in writing that section 35A may apply; failure makes them jointly and severally liable for the unwithheld tax up to the fee they earned. A worked example shows why nobody gambles: on a R4.5 million sale by an individual, the exposure is the R337,500 that should have been withheld, and an agent who earned 6% commission of R270,000 can lose effectively all of it, while the buyer's liability for the full amount survives everyone else's silence. Professionals in the chain are betting their own money on the residency declaration, which is why it appears in the first week of every conveyancing file.

What sellers should expect in practice:

- **A residency declaration early:** the conveyancer screens tax residency before drafting, not at registration.
- **Withholding held from proceeds:** the amount never touches your account; it moves from the [conveyancer's trust account](/guides/conveyancing-fees-cape-town/) to SARS within 21 days.
- **No room for creative answers:** disclosing non-resident status upfront keeps the timeline intact; concealment unravels at the bank in any event.

Buyers purchasing from any seller with a foreign address should raise the section themselves if nobody else has.

## How does the withholding interact with your final tax bill?

Section 35A ends where capital gains tax begins: at assessment, SARS sets the withheld amount against the CGT actually due on the sale and settles the difference in whichever direction it runs. For an individual, the liability is 40% of the gain entering taxable income at marginal rates, an effective charge of up to 18% of the profit.

Both directions occur routinely in our analysis of seller outcomes, and the arithmetic decides which seller you are:

- **Refund cases:** small gains, losses, and long-held properties with high base costs, where 7.5% of price exceeds 18% of profit; the excess returns after the return is assessed, typically months later.
- **Top-up cases:** large gains on low prices are rare, but a R2.5 million sale with a R1.8 million gain produces CGT near R324,000 against withholding of R187,500, leaving a balance payable.
- **Structure effects:** trusts at 15% withholding and an effective CGT near 36% often land closer to neutral, one reason the [ownership-structure choice](/guides/foreigner-property-tax-south-africa-hub/) belongs at purchase, not exit.

Filing is not optional in any scenario: the withholding is only credited through an assessed return, and an unfiled seller has simply donated the money. The proceeds then face the repatriation step, where SARS compliance is checked again before the bank remits, covered in the [repatriation guide](/guides/repatriating-property-sale-proceeds/).

## What should sellers do about section 35A before signing?

Preparation compresses the entire section 35A timeline into the transfer you were running anyway, and every item below is executable the week you decide to sell. Cape Town Invest treats the directive decision as the first fork in any exit plan above R2 million, and briefs foreign buyers on the section before they ever own.

The pre-signature list:

- **Compute the real gain now:** purchase price, improvement invoices and expected selling costs give the CGT estimate that decides whether NR03 is worth lodging.
- **Confirm your SARS status:** an active tax number and filed returns make both the directive and the later repatriation clearance dramatically faster.
- **Brief the conveyancer on residency upfront:** disclosure on day one keeps the 21-day payment mechanics inside the normal registration flow.
- **Match the withholding to your bond:** where settlement plus commission plus withholding approaches the price, the directive is cash-flow critical, not merely efficient.

Insider tip: keep a single exit file from the day you buy, holding the inward transfer record, every improvement invoice and each year's assessment. Sellers with that file lodge a directive in days and clear repatriation in weeks; sellers without it reconstruct 7 years of paperwork at exactly the moment the clock costs money.

## Buyer scenarios when section 35A affects your cash flow

Trust sellers face 15% withholding against an effective CGT rate near 36%, often closer to neutral than individuals. Individual sellers with large gains and low base cost may owe a top-up after assessment. Scenario: R5 million sale, R1 million gain, individual seller. Withholding at 7.5% equals R375,000; CGT near R180,000 yields a refund path after assessment, but only if returns are filed.

Scenario for bonded sellers: bond settlement, commission and withholding together approach gross proceeds. An NR03 directive reducing withholding from 7.5% to match expected CGT can prevent a shortfall on registration day. Without the directive, the conveyancer holds extra from proceeds or the seller tops up trust, delaying payout.

The decision framework is to model three numbers before accepting an offer: expected CGT, statutory withholding, and net cash after bond and commission. If withholding exceeds expected tax by more than R100,000, lodge NR03 early. If expected tax exceeds withholding, budget the top-up before you celebrate the sale price.

Conveyancers must pay withholding within 21 days of registration. Late payment triggers SARS penalties that attach to the seller even when the delay was administrative. Confirm with your attorney that the trust account reserves withholding before disbursing net proceeds to you, especially on tight equity sales.

Worked example for an individual non-resident: sale price R4.8 million, base cost R3.9 million after improvements, selling costs R180,000, gain R720,000. Statutory withholding at 7.5% equals R360,000. CGT near R130,000 after inclusion at marginal rates leaves roughly R230,000 to reclaim after assessment, but only if the return is filed. An NR03 directive lodged six weeks before registration can align the hold to expected tax and release cash at payout instead of lending SARS the difference for a year.

Company sellers at 10% and trusts at 15% face different cash-flow math on the same price. A trust selling at R6 million withholds R900,000 against a CGT bill that may land near R650,000 on a moderate gain. Model entity type before you buy, because the exit withholding rate was set at purchase structure, not at sale negotiation.

Buyers who become sellers within five years should keep every improvement invoice from day one. Kitchen and bathroom upgrades reduce CGT on exit and support a lower NR03 hold if withholding would otherwise exceed tax on a thin gain. Without invoices, SARS treats the base cost as purchase price only, which widens both tax and withholding.

The buyer's conveyancer pays withholding, but the seller chooses whether to lodge NR03. Do not assume the attorney will apply without instruction and a completed gain schedule attached. Send the gain calculation with your mandate pack so the directive can lodge before the first offer arrives.
