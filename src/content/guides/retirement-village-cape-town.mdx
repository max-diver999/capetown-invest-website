  - question: "What is a life right in a South African retirement village?"
    answer: "A life right is the right to occupy a unit for the rest of your life, bought under the Housing Development Schemes for Retired Persons Act 65 of 1988. You do not take transfer of the property and your name does not go on a title deed. On death or when you leave, the scheme buys the right back on the terms in the contract, which commonly returns the original amount plus or minus a defined share of any growth, less a stated deduction. It is a housing product with an estate consequence, not an investment."
  - question: "Is a life right cheaper than buying the unit?"
    answer: "Usually at entry and not always over time. A life right often has a lower entry price than sectional title in the same village and no transfer duty, because there is no transfer, and monthly levies can be lower where the operator carries more of the cost. What you give up is capital growth: a sectional title owner keeps whatever the unit appreciates by, while a life right holder gets back whatever the contract says, which is frequently the original price with a capped share of growth or none at all."
  - question: "Can a foreigner buy into a Cape Town retirement village?"
    answer: "Yes for sectional title, on the same basis as any other property, with no surcharge and no visa needed to own. A life right is a contract rather than a transfer, so the question there is what the specific scheme's rules require, and some are restricted to residents or to occupants who meet an age and residence test. Living in the unit is a separate matter from owning it, and that is where a retirement or financially independent visa comes in."
  - question: "What age do you have to be for a Cape Town retirement village?"
    answer: "It varies by scheme and is set by the scheme's own rules rather than by a single national number. Common thresholds sit at 50 years or 60 years for the primary occupant, with provision for a younger spouse. The rules also usually govern who may inherit occupancy, whether the unit can be let, and what happens if a resident's care needs change. Read the rules rather than the brochure, because these clauses decide what happens in the years the brochure does not picture."
  - question: "Do retirement villages include frail care?"
    answer: "Some do and some do not, and the difference matters more than any other feature. Quick answer: three tenures are sold in the same brochures and they are different assets. Sectional title is ownership, with a title deed, capital growth and transfer duty. A life right under Act 65 of 1988 buys occupation for life with no transfer and a contractually defined return to your estate. Rental buys flexibility and no capital. The choice is an estate decision as much as a housing one.

## What are you actually buying?

One of three things, and the brochure often does not make it obvious. The differences show up at entry, monthly, and at the end, and a buyer choosing on the monthly figure alone can pick the wrong one twice over.

| | Sectional title | Life right | Rental |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title deed in your name | Yes | No | No |
| Tax on entry | Transfer duty above R1,210,000 | None, there is no transfer | None |
| Entry price | Highest of the three | Usually lower | Deposit only |
| Capital growth | Yours | Per the contract, often capped or nil | None |
| Monthly cost | Levy, rates, insurance | Levy, often bundled | Rent |
| What your estate receives | The unit, at market value | The contractual buy-back amount | Nothing |
| Who carries major repairs | The body corporate, funded by owners | Usually the operator | The landlord |

The row that decides most purchases is the second-to-last. A sectional title unit is an asset your estate inherits and can sell at whatever the market pays. A life right returns a number defined years earlier in a contract, and that number is frequently the original price with a capped share of growth, or the original price less a deduction. Neither is wrong; they are different products and they should be priced as such.

## How does a life right actually work?

You pay a capital amount for the right to occupy a specific unit for the rest of your life, under the Housing Development Schemes for Retired Persons Act 65 of 1988. The property stays in the scheme's name, your right is recorded and protected under that Act, and when you die or move out the scheme buys the right back.

The Act exists because this is a vulnerable purchase, and it imposes protections rather than leaving the buyer to the contract alone. What it does not do is set the buy-back formula, which is commercial and varies scheme to scheme. Three shapes are common: the original amount returned with no growth, the original amount plus a defined share of any increase, and the original amount less a stated percentage retained by the scheme.

Insider tip: ask for a worked example of the buy-back on a unit that has actually turned over in the last two years, with the figures. A formula in a contract is abstract; a real case shows how long the buy-back took to pay out, which is the question an executor will face rather than the buyer.

## What does the levy cover, and how has it moved?

Everything the resident does not pay separately, which in a well-run village is a lot: security, gardens, maintenance, communal facilities, and often some level of on-site nursing or emergency response. That breadth is the point of the model and it is also why the levy is the number to interrogate rather than the entry price.

Three questions produce a usable answer:

1. **What is inside the levy and what is billed separately?** Frail care, meals, transport and utilities are sometimes inside and sometimes not.
2. **What has the levy done over three years?** A levy rising 12% a year against inflation nearer 5% is telling you something about the cost base or the occupancy, and three years of statements shows it where one does not.
3. **Is there a reserve fund, and what is in it?** A sectional title scheme must keep a ten-year maintenance plan alongside a reserve, and a thin reserve on ageing communal buildings means a special levy is coming.

The [sectional title levies guide](/guides/sectional-title-levies-cape-town/) sets out how levies are apportioned by participation quota, which applies in the sectional title case, and the [body corporate due diligence guide](/guides/body-corporate-due-diligence-cape-town/) lists the documents that answer the third question.

## Does the village handle the whole arc?

This is the question that separates a good decision from a brochure decision, and it has almost nothing to do with the apartment. A person buying at 65 is buying for the next 25 years, and the needs at 85 are not the needs at 65.

A person buying at 65 has a planning horizon of 20 to 30 years, and South African life expectancy at that age keeps rising, so the arithmetic is not theoretical. Ask whether the village offers independent living only, or independent living with assisted living and frail care on site. Then ask the harder version: is frail care guaranteed to residents, or offered subject to availability. Those are very different promises, and a village with a waiting list is one where a resident whose health changes may have to move out at exactly the point when moving is hardest.

A second question follows from it. If a resident moves from an independent unit into frail care, what happens to the unit and to the levy, and does the family carry both. Schemes answer this differently and the answer belongs in the decision rather than in the future.

## What about a foreign buyer?

Sectional title is straightforward: a non-resident buys, registers and owns on the same basis as a South African, with no surcharge and no visa requirement to hold the deed. Financing is the usual constraint, with banks typically capping non-residents near 50% loan-to-value, though a large share of retirement purchases are cash, which removes the 50% cap as a constraint entirely.

A life right is different because it is a contract rather than a transfer, so eligibility is set by the scheme's own rules and some restrict it. Ask directly rather than assuming.

Living in the unit is a separate question from owning it, and that is where the visa routes come in. The [retirement visa guide](/guides/retirement-visa-south-africa-property/) covers the retired person's route and its income test, and the [financially independent permit guide](/guides/financially-independent-visa-south-africa/) covers the alternative. A UK buyer should read those alongside the [retiring to South Africa page](/segments/cape-town-property-for-uk-retirees/), which sets out the currency and home-country tax position.

## Where are Cape Town's retirement villages?

Concentrated in the Southern Suburbs, the Helderberg and the Winelands rather than on the Atlantic Seaboard, for the same reason as most volume housing: the model needs land. Somerset West and the Helderberg towns carry a particularly high concentration, helped by a flatter setting and proximity to medical facilities.

That geography has an effect worth naming. A village in Somerset West or Durbanville sits in a corridor with a lower entry price than the coast, which is part of why the product exists there, and it also sits further from the CBD and from family who may live in the city.
