Nine corridors cover almost every purchase decision in the metro and the near hinterland: the Atlantic Seaboard, the City Bowl, the Southern Suburbs, the Northern Suburbs, Blaauwberg and the West Coast, False Bay and the Southern Peninsula, the Helderberg, the Cape Winelands, and the Overberg coast.     answer: "The Northern Suburbs, on both entry price and rand per square metre. Durbanville, the premium end of that corridor, carries tickets from about R2.0 million, and the older western end at Parow and Goodwood sits below that. The Atlantic Seaboard is the most expensive, starting at R55,000 per square metre in Sea Point and running past R180,000 in Clifton and Bantry Bay.   - question: "Where do most foreign buyers buy in Cape Town?"

Those two corridors recorded R11.3bn of combined residential sales turnover in 2025, of which about R2.8bn, roughly 25% of value, came from foreign buyers. ## How is Cape Town actually divided?

Nine corridors, and each is defined by a physical feature rather than by an administrative line. Mountain, coast and the N1 do the sorting, which is why suburbs inside a corridor behave alike and suburbs across a boundary do not, even when they sit two kilometres apart.

| Corridor | Runs from, to | Indicative entry | Character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Atlantic Seaboard | Mouille Point to Llandudno | R55,000 psqm | Coastal, scarce, most expensive |
| City Bowl | The CBD up to Vredehoek | R55,000 psqm | Central, sorted by altitude |
| Southern Suburbs | Woodstock to Steenberg | R40,000 psqm | Inland school belt |
| Northern Suburbs | Goodwood to Kraaifontein | lowest in the metro | Value, freehold, N1 corridor |
| Blaauwberg and West Coast | Milnerton to Melkbosstrand | R32,000 psqm | Beach at a discount, family stock |
| False Bay and Southern Peninsula | Muizenberg to Kommetjie | varies widely | Lifestyle coast, longest commute |
| Helderberg | Somerset West to Gordon's Bay | varies widely | Coastal town feel, estate stock |
| Cape Winelands | Stellenbosch to Wellington | varies widely | Estate and village, semigration draw |
| Overberg and far West Coast | Hermanus, Langebaan, Yzerfontein | varies widely | Second homes and retirement |

## Which suburb names are most often confused?

More than you would expect, and the confusion costs real money when a buyer underwrites the wrong address. Several Cape Town suburb names are shared with Johannesburg suburbs, and several Cape Town addresses are marketed under a neighbouring suburb's name because it sells better.

| Name | The Cape Town address | What it is confused with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newlands | Southern Suburbs, cricket and rugby grounds | Newlands in Johannesburg, a different market entirely |
| Claremont | Southern Suburbs retail hub | Claremont in Johannesburg |
| Kensington | Goodwood, Northern Suburbs | Kensington in Johannesburg, far better known |
| Wynberg | Southern Suburbs, village and east side | Wynberg in Sandton |
| Observatory | Southern Suburbs, UCT and hospital belt | Observatory in Johannesburg |
| Constantia | Southern Suburbs green belt | The Constantia wine route, a tourism brand covering several farms |
| Salt River and Woodstock | Filed City Bowl by the City, Southern Suburbs by agents | Themselves, in two different search filters |
| Southern Suburbs | The inland school belt | The Southern Peninsula, a different coast entirely |

Two consequences follow. First, a national property search that is not pinned to the Western Cape will return Johannesburg stock for at least five of the names above, and the prices are not comparable. Second, a development can be marketed under an address it does not occupy: [Zero2One](/projects/zero2one-sea-point/) carries Sea Point in a great deal of agent material and is a tower on Adderley Street in the CBD, which is a different letting market with different rents.

Insider tip: before you compare two listings, check the suburb code or the erf number rather than the suburb name. The City's own property records are the arbiter, and they resolve every one of the collisions above in a way that a listing headline does not.

## What changes when you cross a corridor boundary?

Four things move at once, which is why corridors are a more useful unit than suburbs. Two suburbs on opposite sides of a boundary can sit two kilometres apart and answer completely different investment questions.

**The tenant pool changes.** A City Bowl flat lets to a professional or a visitor; a Southern Suburbs house lets to a family on an academic-year lease; a Northern Suburbs house lets to a local household paying from local salaries. Each pool renews on a different cycle and tolerates a different rent, and a vacancy in one behaves nothing like a vacancy in another.

**The cost stack changes.** Sectional title dominates the Atlantic Seaboard and the City Bowl, so a levy and a body corporate sit between the owner and the property. Freehold dominates the Northern Suburbs and much of the Southern Suburbs, so the owner carries maintenance directly and answers to nobody. Neither is cheaper in the abstract, but they fail in different ways: a levy surprises you by vote, a roof surprises you by weather.

**The buyer pool changes, and with it the sensitivity to interest rates.** The coastal corridors carry a large share of cash and offshore-funded purchases, so a rate move barely registers. The inland corridors are financed locally, so the same move changes what a household can borrow and therefore what a house is worth. The [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-cape-town/) covers the financing side for buyers coming from outside South Africa.

**The regulatory exposure changes.** Nightly letting rules bite hardest where nightly letting is dense, which means the Atlantic Seaboard and the City Bowl feel a by-law change first and the Northern Suburbs barely feel it at all. Our [short-term letting rules guide](/guides/short-term-rental-rules-cape-town/) tracks what the City has proposed and where it lands.

## How should you check whether a suburb is safe?

We publish no safety ranking, and the reason is a data problem rather than a reluctance to answer. SAPS reports crime by police precinct, precincts do not align with suburb boundaries, and a single precinct routinely covers streets with very different conditions. A number attached to a suburb name would look authoritative and mean very little.

Four checks give a better answer than any ranking would:

1. **Find the police precinct that covers the address, then read the SAPS quarterly release for that precinct.** Precinct is the unit the data actually exists in, so start there rather than with the suburb name.
2. **Contact the neighbourhood watch or community policing forum.** They operate at street level, which is the resolution the question is really being asked at, and they will tell you which blocks differ from the precinct average.
3. **Walk the route from the property to the nearest shops after dark.** Lighting, foot traffic and sight lines decide how a place feels, and none of them appear in a statistic.
4. **Ask what the property already spends on security.** Armed response contracts, beams, and whether the street has a paid patrol are all facts a seller can produce, and the answer tells you what the current owner concluded.

Insider tip: ask the agent for the previous three years of the property's own security spend rather than for an opinion about the suburb. A house whose owner pays for a private patrol on top of armed response is telling you something the suburb average never will.

## Where does the metro actually end?

At two different lines, and buyers keep using the wrong one. The City of Cape Town municipality reaches from Atlantis in the north to Simon's Town in the south, but the commuting metro, meaning the area from which people routinely drive to the CBD or Bellville for work, stops well inside that.

The practical boundary sits at Somerset West in the east and Melkbosstrand in the north. Beyond those, a purchase changes character: Stellenbosch, Paarl and Franschhoek run their own local economies and their own rental markets, and Hermanus, Langebaan and Yzerfontein are second-home markets where the letting year is seasonal rather than continuous. That matters to an investor because a vacancy in a seasonal market is not a few weeks, it is the off season, and the yield arithmetic has to be written on eight months rather than twelve.

The [Stellenbosch guide](/guides/stellenbosch-property-investment-guide/) covers the Winelands case, where the local economy is strong enough that the seasonal caveat does not apply in the same way.
