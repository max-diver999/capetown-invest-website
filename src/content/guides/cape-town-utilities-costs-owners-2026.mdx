## What are Cape Town's utility tariff increases for 2026/27?

The adopted 2026/27 budget sets the annual increases every owner pays from 1 July 2026: water and sanitation up 4.5%, refuse collection up 3.75%, and electricity up an average of 6.64%, which the City states is below the Eskom-approved national municipal increase. Cape Town Invest tracks the stack because it moves net yield directly.

| Service | 2026/27 change | Structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electricity | +6.64% average | Home User tariffs, mostly prepaid; below national benchmark |
| Water and sanitation | +4.5% | Block tariff on usage plus fixed charge by meter size |
| Refuse collection | +3.75% | Flat service charge per bin service |
| City-wide cleaning charge | Scrapped | Declared unlawful by the High Court, April 2026 |

Increases compound with the rates side, where GV2025 values took effect on the same day with a rate-in-rand of roughly 0.0064, covered in the [GV2025 rates guide](/guides/cape-town-municipal-valuation-objection-gv/). Budget both together: the July 2026 municipal bill is a different document from June's.

## What did the court ruling change about fixed charges?

The April 2026 ruling of the Western Cape High Court means fixed charges can no longer scale with property value: the court declared the City's fixed water, sanitation and cleaning charges, all calculated on property value bands, unlawful, and required their removal within 3 months. The 2026/27 budget implements exactly that.

The practical changes on your bill:

- **Cleaning charge:** removed entirely, worth hundreds of rand a month on high-value homes.
- **Fixed water and sanitation:** now tied to water-meter size, roughly R400 to R600 monthly on a standard 15mm to 20mm residential connection, identical for a R2 million and a R20 million home on the same meter.
- **Rates absorb the gap:** the City recovered part of the lost revenue through the rates system while raising the rates-free portion to R620,000.

Our analysis puts the biggest winners on the Atlantic Seaboard, where value-banded fixed charges hit hardest: a R15 million home that paid top-band fixed charges in 2025/26 now pays the same meter-based charge as a suburban house, a swing worth thousands of rand a year. The principle travels too, since charges that scale with value must now pass through the rates process, with its exemptions and public participation, rather than arrive as tariffs.

## What does the full owner cost stack look like in 2026/27?

The full holding cost of a Cape Town investment property is the sum of five lines, and only two of them stop when a tenant moves in. A worked example on a R3,000,000 sectional title flat, the modal foreign-buyer purchase, shows where the money goes each month.

| Cost line | Modelled monthly | Who ultimately carries it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Municipal rates (after R620,000 exemption) | R1,270 | Owner, always |
| Body corporate levy | R2,500 to R4,500 | Owner, always |
| Fixed water and sanitation charges | R400 to R600 | Owner in practice |
| Metered electricity | R800 to R1,500 | Tenant on prepaid; owner when vacant or short-let |
| Metered water usage | R300 to R700 | Tenant via lease recovery; owner when vacant |

Vacant, the unit consumes R4,500 to R6,500 a month; tenanted on a long lease, the owner's fixed share is roughly R4,200 to R5,400. Levy mechanics, including special levies that arrive on top, sit in the [sectional title levies guide](/guides/sectional-title-levies-cape-town/), and the rates arithmetic is worked in the [rates and taxes guide](/guides/cape-town-rates-taxes-property/).

## How do utilities change your rental yield?

Utilities and fixed charges typically cost a Cape Town landlord 1 to 2 percentage points of gross yield, and the erosion is invisible in every listing advert. Gross yield is rent over price; running yield is what remains after the owner's share of the monthly stack, and the gap is where underwriting goes wrong.

The worked example, on the same R3,000,000 flat letting at R18,000 a month: gross yield is 7.2%, but the owner's fixed share of roughly R4,700 a month, rates, levy and fixed charges, absorbs R56,400 a year, dragging the running yield to about 5.3% before management, insurance, maintenance or a single week of vacancy. Push the same unit into nightly letting and the owner reclaims no lines at all: electricity, water, fibre and cleaning all revert to the host, which is why short-let gross figures of 13% to 19% in the [Airbnb yield table](/guides/airbnb-yields-by-suburb-cape-town/) shrink by a third to a half on the way to net. A long-let tenant on a well-drafted lease carries metered usage; a vacancy or a guest carries nothing. Cape Town Invest underwrites every purchase at the running-yield line, not the advert line.

The quick conversion rules:

- **Long-let:** subtract 1 to 2 points from gross for the owner's fixed stack.
- **Short-let:** subtract a third to a half of gross for the full cost load.
- **Any model:** one vacant month returns the entire stack to the owner.

Full method and suburb figures sit in the [rental yield guide](/guides/cape-town-rental-yield-guide/).

## How does Cape Town's water system affect owners in 2026?

Cape Town's water pricing is still the architecture of the 2018 drought: a steep block tariff that rewards low usage, near-universal metering, and a system rebuilt to survive scarcity. Day Zero never arrived, but its tariff structure remains the framework every owner pays within in 2026/27, now 4.5% higher across water and sanitation.

What that structure means in practice:

1. **Block tariffs punish heavy usage:** consumption above the first blocks prices at multiples of the entry rate, so gardens and pools are the expensive part of ownership.
2. **Meter size sets fixed charges:** after the court ruling, the 15mm or 20mm connection defines the fixed line, worth checking on any freehold purchase.
3. **Sectional title splits differ:** schemes with bulk meters recover water through levies or participation quotas, so two identical flats can carry different water lines.
4. **Backup matters at the top end:** boreholes, wellpoints and tanks add resilience and carry their own compliance, a factor covered in the [water security guide](/guides/cape-town-water-security-property/).

Insider tip: on freehold due diligence, pull 12 months of municipal statements rather than the seller's summary. A single leak on the owner's side of the meter can run the water line to 5 figures in a quarter, and the account history shows it instantly.

## What should investors budget for the next 3 years?

Utility budgeting for 2026 through 2029 is a compounding exercise, because tariffs rise annually while the GV2025 roll holds rates values fixed until June 2029. Foreign buyers who model this year's bill as flat for a 3 to 5 year hold understate the cost line by a widening margin every July.

The planning assumptions Cape Town Invest applies:

- **Electricity:** assume 6% to 8% annual increases, tracking the national trajectory even when Cape Town undercuts it.
- **Water, sanitation, refuse:** assume 4% to 6% annually, with structure stable after the court ruling.
- **Rates:** value fixed to June 2029 on GV2025; the rate-in-rand moves with each budget, so assume small annual drift.
- **Levies:** assume 8% to 10% annual increases on sectional title, the corpus-wide pattern as schemes rebuild reserves.

Compounded, a R4,700 monthly fixed stack in 2026 becomes roughly R5,400 by mid-2028 on mid-range assumptions, a drift of about 15% that a 3-year cash-flow model must carry from day one. Levies do the most damage inside that number: at 9% annual escalation a R3,000 levy reaches R3,565 within 2 years, while the rates line stays comparatively tame because the GV2025 value is frozen and only the rate-in-rand drifts. Buyers comparing two similar flats should request the actual municipal account and levy schedule for each, then run this table forward for the full hold: our review of mid-market sectional title stock keeps finding spreads of R800 to R1,200 a month between near-identical units in neighbouring schemes, which is R29,000 to R43,000 over 3 years, regularly wider than the gap between their asking prices. The cheap-looking flat with the expensive scheme is the worse buy.

## Buyer scenarios for utility and rates budgeting

Long-let investors should model tenant recovery separately from owner-paid fixed lines. In many Sea Point schemes the levy includes bulk water and refuse, while electricity stays on the tenant meter. A buyer who adds full utility stack to the levy double-counts. Scenario: R3,800 levy quoted by the agent plus R1,200 municipal account equals R5,000, but the tenant pays R900 of electricity and R400 of water, so owner net is R3,700 not R5,000.

Short-let operators face the commercial rates scenario under the draft by-law. A De Waterkant loft billed residential today at R42,000 a year might jump above R57,000 when reclassified, erasing most of a half-point of gross yield. Underwrite both cases before offer.

Freehold buyers in the Atlantic Seaboard belt should inspect meter size and backup water. A 20mm connection carries higher fixed water charges after the 2026 court ruling than a 15mm line, and borehole compliance adds capex. Scenario: two Hout Bay homes at the same price, one with compliant borehole and 15mm meter, one with failed wellpoint and leak history. The second carries hidden R80,000 to R120,000 over three years.

Foreign owners absent six months a year should appoint someone to open municipal correspondence and pay accounts. Arrears interest and disconnection notices accumulate silently and delay transfer when you sell. The decision framework is simple: treat utilities like bond payments, automated and monitored, not like optional admin.

Sectional title buyers should compare participation quotas, not just levies. Two units with identical levies can carry different water recoveries if quotas differ. Request the schedule of participation quotas and the last audited financials before offer. A scheme with weak reserves often announces special levies that hit utilities budgeting harder than tariff rises.

Compare winter and summer municipal accounts separately on Atlantic Seaboard freehold. Garden irrigation and pool top-ups in January can double the water line against July, while electricity stays relatively flat if the tenant carries prepaid. A buyer who annualises one summer bill understates cost; a buyer who annualises winter alone overstates it. Pull twelve consecutive months or accept a modeled band of R900 to R1,400 monthly variance on a typical three-bedroom Hout Bay home with pool.

Vacant letting seasons return the full stack to the owner. If you underwrite short-let on peak-season occupancy only, stress-test two empty months where you still pay rates near R1,270, levy R3,200, fixed water R500 and insurance R800 with zero guest income. That scenario is common in May and June, and it separates robust yields from marketing screenshots.

Prepaid electricity on tenant meters still leaves the owner with rates, levy and fixed water in sectional title. Confirm whether the levy includes sewerage recovery or bills it separately on the municipal account, because some Green Point schemes split lines in ways agents do not mention on viewing.

Landlords recovering utilities from tenants should mirror the lease to municipal billing cycles. A tenant who pays prepaid electricity but ignores owner-paid fixed water creates arrears that block your rates clearance on sale. Ask the managing agent how the scheme splits bulk water before you sign the lease template. On freehold, confirm whether the borehole pump shares the municipal meter or runs on a separate account. Pool backwash and irrigation lines are the usual leak suspects on older Camps Bay and Hout Bay homes when a summer bill doubles without tenant change. Fix leaks before listing for sale; buyers pull twelve months of accounts in due diligence.
