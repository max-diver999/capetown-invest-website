Quick answer: **occupational rent** is what the occupier pays the owner between the occupation date and registration at the Deeds Office. South African sales separate those dates routinely, so the gap is normal rather than exceptional. There is no statutory formula: the amount is negotiated in the [Offer to Purchase](/guides/offer-to-purchase-south-africa/), with market practice often quoted near **1%** of the purchase price per month.

## Two dates, not one

A South African purchase has an occupation date and a registration date, and they are rarely the same day. Occupation is when you may move in. Registration is when the Deeds Office records the property in your name and ownership actually changes.

Between those two points somebody is living in, or entitled to live in, a property somebody else still owns. Occupational rent is the compensation for that. It is not a penalty and not a deposit; it is rent for the period of occupation without ownership.

## Who pays

| Situation | Who occupies | Who pays whom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyer moves in before registration | Buyer | Buyer pays the seller |
| Seller stays on after registration | Seller | Seller pays the new owner |
| Occupation tied to registration date | Neither gap arises | No occupational rent |

The third row is worth noticing. If the Offer to Purchase sets occupation to coincide with registration, no occupational rent arises at all. That is often the cleanest structure for a buyer who is not in the country and has no interest in moving in early.

## How the amount is set

South African law does not prescribe a figure. The amount is agreed between the parties and written into the agreement of sale.

| Reference point | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| About 1% of purchase price per month | The most commonly quoted market convention |
| Roughly 0.6% to 0.8% per month | Also seen, particularly where the parties negotiate |
| Market rental for a comparable property | Used where the parties prefer an evidenced figure |
| Pro rata by days | How a part month is normally apportioned |

All of these are conventions, not rules. A buyer who treats 1% as a legal requirement has misread the position, and so has a seller who insists on it as though it were. On a R5,000,000 property, 1% a month is R50,000 a month, which over a transfer that slips towards 4 months is a real number rather than a rounding item.

## What it does and does not cover

Occupational rent is generally a flat amount for occupation. Utilities, water and electricity, are normally for the occupant's account, and rates and taxes normally stay with the owner unless the agreement provides otherwise.

Because these are conventions, the agreement has to say so. A one-line occupational rent clause that names an amount and nothing else leaves four questions unanswered: who pays utilities, who pays rates, who is responsible for maintenance during the period, and what happens if the occupant damages something before ownership passes.

## The overseas buyer problem

This is where the term stops being administrative and starts costing money.

If your occupation date is a fixed calendar date and registration runs long, you are liable from that date whether or not you are in South Africa. A buyer in London with an occupation date in March and a registration that lands in May pays two months of occupational rent on a flat nobody has slept in.

A normal Cape Town transfer runs 8 to 12 weeks, roughly 2 to 3 months. When something goes wrong, a lost title deed, municipal arrears, or a bond cancellation notice that was never given, it stretches towards 16 weeks or past 4 months. The [transfer timeline guide](/guides/property-transfer-timeline-delays/) sets out where that time goes.

Two ways to handle it, both decided before signature:

| Structure | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Occupation tied to registration | No occupational rent, no exposure to transfer delay |
| Fixed occupation date | Rent runs from that date regardless of where you are |

If the seller needs a fixed date, the negotiation is about the monthly figure and about who carries the risk of a delay neither party caused.

## Advantages and disadvantages of each structure

The choice is made once, at signature, and it is worth making deliberately.

| Structure | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Occupation tied to registration | No occupational rent at all; no exposure to a transfer that slips from 2 to 3 months towards 4 months; nothing to negotiate about utilities or rates | You cannot move in, renovate or let the property until registration; a seller who needs to vacate on a fixed date may refuse |
| Fixed occupation date, buyer occupies early | You get the keys on a known date; renovation or letting can start before registration | Rent runs from that date regardless of delay or of whether you are in the country; at 1% a month on a R5,000,000 home that is R50,000 a month |
| Seller stays on after registration | The seller gets time to move; often unlocks a price concession | You own a property you cannot use; recovering rent from a seller who has already been paid out is harder than withholding it |

For an owner-occupier already in Cape Town, a fixed occupation date is usually worth the rent. For an investor buying from overseas with no intention of moving in, tying occupation to registration removes a cost with no offsetting benefit.

## Where it sits in the transaction

Occupational rent is agreed in the Offer to Purchase, alongside the price, the bond clause and the fixtures list. It is not something the conveyancer adds later, and it is not adjusted by the Deeds Office.
