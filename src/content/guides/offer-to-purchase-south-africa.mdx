Quick answer: the South African **Offer to Purchase** is the sale agreement, not a preliminary step. Sign it and the seller accepts, and you are contractually bound. The five-day cooling-off right under section 29A of the Alienation of Land Act applies only where the price is **R250,000 or less** and the buyer is a natural person, which excludes essentially every Cape Town purchase. Your real exit is a **suspensive condition**, usually the bond clause. ## The OTP is the contract, not the conversation before it

Buyers arriving from other markets often read "offer" as a negotiating step that becomes a contract later. In South Africa it does not work that way. The Alienation of Land Act requires a sale of land to be in writing and signed by both parties, and the Offer to Purchase is that writing. When the seller signs your offer, the sale exists.

That single fact changes how you should treat the document. There is no later contract to fix a term you did not read. The price, the deposit, the occupation date, the fixtures included, the deadline for your bond, and the consequences of missing it are all settled the moment the seller countersigns.

## The cooling-off right almost certainly does not cover you

Section 29A of the Alienation of Land Act 68 of 1981 does give a purchaser five days to revoke an offer in writing. It is narrow.

| Condition | What it requires |
| --- | --- |
| Price ceiling | Purchase price of R250,000 or less |
| Buyer type | A natural person, not a company or trust |
| Property type | Residential land, sold outside a publicly advertised auction |
| Excluded cases | Parties already contracted on substantially identical terms; buyer reserved a right of nomination; purchase by exercising an option open for at least five days |
| Form of revocation | Written, signed by the purchaser or an authorised agent, identifying the offer, and unconditional |

Cape Town property prices sit far above the R250,000 ceiling, and many foreign buyers purchase through a company or trust, which excludes them a second time. Treat the cooling-off right as something that exists in the statute and not in your transaction.

A separate five-business-day rescission right exists under the Consumer Protection Act for direct marketing transactions. A normal agency sale is not direct marketing, so it does not help here either.

## Suspensive conditions are the real exit

Because you cannot simply change your mind, the clauses that let you out are the suspensive conditions. A suspensive condition makes the entire agreement conditional on something happening by a stated date. If it does not happen, the sale lapses and both parties are released.

The common ones:

| Condition | What it protects | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bond approval | A financed buyer who cannot raise the loan | The exact amount, the deadline, and whether partial approval counts |
| Sale of the buyer's existing property | A buyer whose funds are tied up | Whether the seller may keep marketing and accept a better offer |
| Satisfactory inspection or compliance certificates | A buyer discovering defects or missing certificates | Who pays for the certificates and by when |

The bond clause is the one that decides most deals. Two numbers matter: the amount you must be approved for, and the date by which approval must arrive. If the OTP names an amount higher than a bank will lend you, the condition fails and the sale falls away. If it names an amount lower than you actually need, you can be bound to a purchase you cannot fund. A non-resident should set that amount against what South African banks lend foreigners rather than against a local loan-to-value, which the [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-cape-town/) covers.

## Setting the bond deadline against real timelines

A bond clause with an unrealistic deadline is the most common way a sound purchase collapses. Set the date against how the process actually runs rather than against optimism.

| Stage | What the Cape Town corpus records | What it means for your clause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bond approval on a clean file | About 3 weeks | A 2-week bond deadline invites failure on paperwork alone |
| Registration after signature | 8 to 12 weeks, roughly 2 to 3 months | Your occupation date should assume this, not a best case |
| When something goes wrong | 16 weeks, close to 4 months | Build the gap into the occupational rent you can absorb |
| A missed bond cancellation notice, 90 days or about 3 months | Past 4 months | Ask the seller's conveyancer early whether notice has been given |

Two more numbers shape what you can honestly write into the clause. A South African bank will usually lend a non-resident about **50 percent** of the purchase price, with the balance introduced from offshore, which the [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-cape-town/) sets out. And the tax on the price is either transfer duty, which starts above the **R1,210,000** zero-rated threshold, or **15 percent** VAT on a new build from a VAT-registered developer, never both. The OTP should say which one applies to your purchase.


The price line does more than name what you pay the seller. It decides which tax applies and how much, and the [transfer duty explainer](/guides/south-africa-transfer-duty-explained/) carries the full SARS table.

| What the OTP price triggers | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Transfer duty below the zero-rated threshold | 0 percent |
| Transfer duty, first band above it | 3 percent on the slice |
| Transfer duty, top band | 13 percent on the highest slice |
| New build from a VAT-registered developer | 15 percent VAT instead of duty |

Write the price knowing which of those two applies. A buyer who budgets for duty and then finds VAT baked into a developer price has mis-planned the largest once-off cost in the deal.

The date on the OTP also starts a clock on the tax itself: SARS expects transfer duty within 6 months of the sale agreement date, and interest runs after that. On a purchase where registration is already tracking at 2 to 3 months, that leaves less headroom than buyers assume.

## The disclosure form must be attached to your OTP

Section 67 of the Property Practitioners Act 22 of 2019 requires the estate agent to obtain a completed and signed disclosure form from the seller before accepting the mandate, to hand it to a prospective buyer before an offer is made, and to attach it to the sale agreement.

The consequence of a missing form is the useful part. Where no disclosure form is attached to the agreement, the agreement is interpreted as if the seller disclosed no defects whatsoever. That is a poor position for a seller and a strong one for a buyer, which is exactly why you should check whether the form is physically attached to the document you are signing, and read it before you sign rather than after.

## Voetstoots sits next to that duty, not above it

A voetstoots clause sells the property as it stands and puts existing defects on the buyer. It is standard and you should expect it. What it does not do is licence concealment: a seller who knows about a defect and deliberately hides it cannot rely on the clause, and the statutory disclosure duty runs alongside it.

Read the voetstoots wording and the attached disclosure form as one unit. Together they decide who pays when a problem surfaces after transfer, which on Cape Town stock most often means damp, roof condition, electrical compliance, or a body corporate issue on a sectional title unit.

## Occupation date and transfer date are different dates

The OTP names an occupation date, when you may move in, and the transfer happens separately when the Deeds Office registers the property into your name. When those dates differ, the party in occupation pays occupational rent to the other for the gap.

For a buyer overseas this deserves attention, because a registration delay can leave you paying occupational rent on a property you are not using. The [transfer timeline guide](/guides/property-transfer-timeline-delays/) sets out where those delays usually come from.

## The deposit belongs in a trust account

The deposit is whatever the OTP says it is; South African law does not fix a percentage. What is worth insisting on is where it sits. A deposit should be held in the conveyancing attorney's or agency's trust account, with the OTP naming the account and stating who earns the interest during the wait.

For a foreign buyer that paper trail has a second life. Funds brought into South Africa need clean documentation so that the proceeds can be taken out again on a future sale, which connects directly to [FICA verification](/guides/fica-requirements-foreign-property-buyers/) and to the record your conveyancer keeps.

## What to check before you sign

| Clause | The question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Parties and property description | Does it match the title deed and the erf number exactly? |
| Purchase price and what it includes | Is transfer duty or VAT stated, and which applies? |
| Bond clause | What amount, by what date, and what counts as approval? |
| Deposit | How much, into which trust account, and who earns the interest? |
| Fixtures and fittings | Is the list attached, and does it match what you were shown? |
| Disclosure form | Is it completed, signed and physically attached? |
| Occupation and transfer dates | What is the gap, and what is the occupational rent? |
| Compliance certificates | Electrical, gas, plumbing, beetle, and who pays for each? |
| Agent commission | Who pays it and when is it earned? |

Run the price you are about to write into the OTP through the [transfer cost calculator](/guides/transfer-duty-calculator/) before you sign, so the duty, attorney and bond lines are numbers rather than assumptions.
